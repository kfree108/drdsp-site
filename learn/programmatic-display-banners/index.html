Programmatic Display Banners: The Standard Sizes, and Where the Real Waste Hides

Updated 2026-08-21 · 1414 words · Written against what currently ranked for “programmatic display banners”
The short answer

A programmatic display banner is a fixed-dimension visual ad — most commonly the IAB's standard sizes like 300x250, 728x90, or 160x600 — bought through a real-time auction rather than a negotiated placement. The size standard exists so one creative can run across thousands of different publishers without a custom build for each.

Why banner sizes are standardized at all

Before the industry converged on a shared set of dimensions, every publisher's ad slots were sized differently, which meant a creative built for one site often didn't fit another without a redesign — expensive and slow, and the opposite of what programmatic buying is supposed to enable. The IAB's standard ad unit portfolio fixed that: a small set of common sizes — the 300x250 medium rectangle, 728x90 leaderboard, 160x600 wide skyscraper, 300x600 half-page, and 320x50 mobile banner among them — that the large majority of publishers agreed to support. Build one creative at each size and it can run across nearly any publisher's inventory without a custom fit. The 300x250 alone reportedly accounts for over 40% of display impressions on Google's own display network, which is as good a sign as any of how much the standard actually gets used.

Static, animated, and rich media — not the same creative decision

A banner isn't automatically a still image. The same standard sizes accept static JPEGs and PNGs, animated GIFs, and richer HTML5 units that can include interaction, video, or expandable panels. Each carries a different production cost and a different technical constraint — HTML5 units, in particular, typically face a separate weight budget for their initial load versus their total file size, because they keep pulling in assets after the ad first renders, unlike a static image that's fully loaded the moment it appears.

The trade-off is rarely just cost. Rich media and expandable units tend to carry more page weight and a slower load, which some publishers restrict or price differently precisely because it affects the rest of the page's performance, not just the ad slot. A static banner is the safest default for a broad, high-volume programmatic buy across many publishers with different technical requirements; an HTML5 or expandable unit is worth the added production cost mainly on a smaller number of premium, hand-picked placements where the extra interactivity has a real chance of being seen and used rather than scrolled past in a fraction of a second.

A worked example: banner blindness and what it actually predicts

"Banner blindness" — the tendency of experienced web users to subconsciously ignore anything in a recognizable ad-shaped box — is real and well documented, and it's the reason standard banner click-through rates have sat in the low fractions of a percent for years, far below what a first-time internet user in the 1990s produced on the same format. This is worth stating plainly rather than treating as an embarrassing secret: a low banner CTR is not, on its own, evidence a campaign is failing. Banners do a large share of their work through view-through impact — brand recall and consideration — that a click-through metric was never built to capture. Judging a prospecting banner campaign purely on CTR is applying the wrong yardstick to the format, not catching a real problem.

Where the waste actually lives, and it usually isn't the banner shape

The mistake worth naming honestly is spending review time debating banner creative — colors, copy, CTA button placement — while the real waste sits somewhere the creative team never looks: line-item and placement selection. In a 31-day pull across 27 advertisers in our own Amazon DSP book, one in five spending line items produced zero attributed purchases, but between them they burned only 0.7% of the total budget — 259 of 1,325 line items took money and returned nothing, totalling $4,927 of $733,336. That's a strikingly different shape from search-advertising waste, where a losing tail of unchosen search terms can run to a third of spend. On programmatic display, an advertiser chooses every line item, so failures tend to be small, visible, and easy to prune — which means the bigger lever isn't finding the losers, it's making sure review time goes toward the handful of line items carrying most of the spend, not the long tail of small ones that already cost almost nothing when they fail.

What to check before touching the creative

Before redesigning a banner because performance looks weak, check what the banner is actually being asked to do — a prospecting placement judged on last-click conversion will look worse than a retargeting placement running the identical creative, because the audiences are at different points in the funnel. Check spend concentration second, sorting line items by dollars rather than by name, since the handful of large line items usually explain more of the result than the whole long tail combined. Only once both checks are done does the creative itself deserve real scrutiny — and even then, a size or format swap is usually a cheaper first test than a full redesign.

Which one you should actually pick

A brand launching its first programmatic display campaign should start with the IAB standard sizes in static or simple animated form — cheap to produce, broadly compatible, and good enough to test whether the audience and placement strategy work before investing in richer creative. A brand already running programmatic display and unhappy with results is usually better served auditing line-item spend concentration before commissioning a creative refresh.

Common questions

What are the standard programmatic banner ad sizes?

The IAB's most widely supported sizes are 300x250 (medium rectangle), 728x90 (leaderboard), 160x600 (wide skyscraper), 300x600 (half-page), and 320x50 (mobile banner). Building creative at these dimensions lets a single set of assets run across most publisher inventory without a custom fit for each site.

Is a low click-through rate on a banner ad a sign of a failing campaign?

Not automatically. Banner blindness has kept standard display click-through rates in the low fractions of a percent for years, and a large share of a banner's real impact comes through view-through brand recall rather than clicks. A prospecting banner should generally be judged on reach, view-through lift, and downstream conversion, not click-through rate alone.

Should I use static images or HTML5 for programmatic banners?

It depends on the goal. Static JPEG or PNG creative is cheaper to produce and loads instantly. HTML5 allows interaction, motion, or expandable panels, at the cost of a separate, usually stricter file-size budget for its initial load versus total weight — worth the added production cost only when the interactivity is doing real work for the campaign.

Where does programmatic display budget actually get wasted, if not on weak creative?

Usually in line-item and placement selection, not creative. In our own managed accounts, roughly a fifth of spending line items typically account for the large majority of budget, while the losing tail of zero-return line items burns a very small share of total spend — the opposite pattern from search advertising, where unchosen search terms can waste a third of the budget.

Do I need a different banner size for every publisher I advertise on?

Not if you build against the IAB's standard sizes. That's the entire point of the standard — a creative built at 300x250, 728x90, and the handful of other common sizes will fit the large majority of publisher inventory without a custom build for each site, which is what made programmatic banner buying scalable in the first place.

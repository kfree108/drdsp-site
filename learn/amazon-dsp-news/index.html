Which 'Amazon DSP' is in the news — the ad platform or the courier program?

"Amazon DSP" currently means two unrelated things in the news, and mixing them up wastes time. One is the Demand-Side Platform, Amazon's programmatic buying tool for display, video and audio ads. The other is the Delivery Service Partner program, the courier franchise network that delivers Amazon packages.

If you landed here looking for the courier program: Amazon announced a $1.9 billion investment in DSP driver pay and safety tools at its Ignite event, on top of $16.7 billion invested since 2018. Separately, the New Jersey Attorney General filed an antitrust suit in August 2026 alleging Amazon uses its control over the delivery network to suppress driver wages and block DSPs from hiring each other's drivers. Both stories are about trucks and drivers, not ads.

This page is about the advertising Demand-Side Platform. The real news there isn't a dollar figure or a lawsuit — it's a quiet, overdue change in how advertisers are being asked to prove DSP actually did anything.

The real DSP news: last-click attribution is losing its authority

For years, Amazon DSP reporting leaned on last-click and view-through attribution: if a shopper saw or clicked a display ad and bought within the lookback window, the ad got credit. That number was always easy to read and always overstated. It counts shoppers who were already going to buy — display just happened to be the last thing they saw.

The fix isn't a new dashboard. It's a different question: what happens to sales when you take the ad away? Holdout tests answer that by exposing one group and withholding a matched group, then comparing outcomes. Matched-market tests do the same thing at a regional level when a clean holdout isn't practical. Both measure incrementality — the sales that wouldn't have happened without the ad — which last-click cannot do and never could.

The other piece of DSP news that matters: Amazon Marketing Cloud now lets advertisers reconcile DSP and Sponsored Products/Sponsored Brands in one clean room, so a single purchase doesn't get counted as a win twice — once by the display report and once by the search report. Without that reconciliation, a "6x ROAS" DSP campaign and a "4x ROAS" sponsored campaign can both be claiming credit for the same order.

When the DSP numbers come back bad

Sometimes honest measurement gives you bad news: ROAS looks fine on last-click but collapses under a holdout test, or new-to-brand share is low, or AMC reconciliation shows DSP taking credit sponsored ads already earned. That's not a failure of the platform — it's the report finally telling you something true.

  • If incremental lift is near zero: the audience or placement isn't adding sales beyond what search and organic would have delivered anyway. Cut the segment or narrow it, don't just lower the bid.
  • If new-to-brand share is low: the campaign is mostly reaching existing buyers. That can still be worth running for retention, but it shouldn't be sold internally as growth.
  • If AMC shows double-counting: the fix is in the measurement plan, not the media buy. Re-run the reconciliation before touching budgets.

Every change to a live campaign should carry three things before it runs: the evidence behind it, a measurement plan, and a rollback trigger. If the fix doesn't work, you should know within a defined window, not after a quarter has passed.

The mistake almost every advertiser makes with DSP — us included

The most common mistake is treating a DSP dashboard number as proof, because it's a number and numbers feel objective. Last-click ROAS is a real measurement of something — it just isn't a measurement of causation. We've made this mistake ourselves: reporting a strong last-click number before running the holdout that would have shown a smaller, but real, incremental lift.

The second mistake is scaling a test before it's big enough to trust. A holdout test on a small audience can show a lift that's just noise. If you can't tell whether a result would reappear on a second, independent sample, it isn't a result yet — it's an anecdote with a chart around it.

Common questions

Is Amazon DSP the same thing as Amazon's Delivery Service Partner program?

No. Amazon DSP the advertising platform is Amazon's Demand-Side Platform for programmatic display, video and audio buying. Amazon's Delivery Service Partner program is the courier franchise network that delivers packages. They share an acronym and nothing else — different teams, different business, different news cycle entirely.

What changed in Amazon DSP measurement recently?

The shift is toward incrementality testing — holdouts and matched controls — instead of relying on last-click or view-through attribution. Amazon Marketing Cloud now lets advertisers reconcile DSP and sponsored ads in one clean room, so the same purchase doesn't get double-credited by two different reports.

Can I trust last-click ROAS for Amazon DSP at all?

It's a real number, but it's directional, not causal. It's useful for spotting which placements and creatives are getting engagement, but it will systematically overstate DSP's contribution because it credits shoppers who were already going to buy. Pair it with a holdout test before making budget decisions on it alone.

How big does a DSP holdout test need to be to trust the result?

There's no single number that applies to every account — it depends on baseline conversion rate and how large the expected lift is. The practical test is repeatability: if you can't tell whether the result would reappear on an independent sample, treat it as a signal to retest, not a conclusion to act on.

What does 'new-to-brand' percentage tell me that ROAS doesn't?

ROAS tells you sales happened. New-to-brand percentage tells you whether those sales came from people who hadn't bought from you before. A campaign with strong ROAS but a low new-to-brand share is mostly re-selling to existing customers — a real result, but a different one than growth.

Two Businesses, One Confusing Name

If you searched this wanting to become a delivery contractor — vans, routes, drivers — you're after Amazon's Delivery Service Partner program, a logistics franchise with its own capital requirements, vehicle leasing terms and driver payroll. Those figures change year to year and vary by market; check Amazon's own DSP partner page for current numbers rather than any secondhand summary, including this one.

This page is about the other Amazon DSP: the demand-side platform brands use to buy programmatic display, video and audio ads, on Amazon and off it. Same three letters, completely different business. Nothing below applies to running a delivery fleet.

What Amazon DSP (the Ad Platform) Actually Buys

Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands and Sponsored Display run on auctions triggered by a search term or a product page — someone is already looking for something. Amazon DSP works upstream of that. It buys audiences, not keywords: shoppers who viewed a product, abandoned a cart, bought a competitor, or match a lookalike profile, served with display, streaming video or audio across Amazon-owned properties like Fire TV, Twitch and IMDb, plus the open exchange.

That makes it a strong fit for brands with a considered purchase or a retargeting funnel worth building — cookware, outdoor gear, grooming, gardening, kits and subscriptions. Brands like Ridge, Beardbrand or Epic Gardening sit in that category: repeat-purchase or high-consideration categories where showing up before someone searches again is worth paying for. It's a weak fit for a single low-margin SKU with no repeat behavior behind it — the media minimums and measurement overhead don't pay back.

What the Numbers Actually Look Like — A Worked Example

Numbers help more than definitions do. Across 30 advertisers running Amazon DSP in July 2026, the book behind this page delivered 78.4 million impressions at a blended $4.00 CPM, a $1.42 blended cost-per-click, and a 6.04x return on ad spend. That's an average across 30 accounts of different sizes and different mixes of display, video and audio — not one campaign's best week.

Underneath that ROAS: 57,137 attributed purchases at a blended $5.49 cost-per-acquisition, and 20.1% of those purchases came from shoppers who'd never bought the brand before. That new-to-brand share is the number worth watching, because it's the part sponsored ads structurally can't show you — sponsored ads only fire once someone's already searching.

Read the chain in order: impressions buy attention, CPC tells you what that attention cost to turn into a click, CPA tells you what a sale cost once someone did, and ROAS is the summary everyone asks for first — but it's the least useful number on its own, because it hides whether the return came from incremental sales or from shoppers who'd have bought anyway.

Why Last-Click Can't Answer the Question You're Actually Asking

Last-click attribution hands full credit to whichever ad a shopper clicked right before buying. DSP frequently shows someone an ad days before they search and click a Sponsored Product — the sale gets credited to search, and DSP looks like it did nothing, even though it started the chain. The reverse happens too: DSP gets credited for a purchase it merely rode along with.

The only way to answer "did display add sales that wouldn't have happened anyway" is a holdout: split comparable audiences, expose one group to DSP, withhold it from a matched control, then compare purchase rates. Amazon Marketing Cloud is where that reconciliation happens — the one place DSP and sponsored ads stop double-counting each other, because you can see both exposure paths against the same shopper instead of trusting whichever channel's own dashboard claims the sale.

None of this is a knock on last-click as a concept. It's fast and free, and it's fine for search. It was never built to measure a channel that works by showing up before anyone searches at all.

The Mistake Almost Everyone Makes (Us Included)

The most common mistake is judging DSP by its own dashboard's last-click ROAS and cutting it the first slow month — usually right as a holdout would have shown the opposite. We've made this call ourselves on a newer account: cut spend based on a soft same-platform report, then ran the matched-control test a quarter later and found the cut had removed real, if slower-to-attribute, incremental sales.

The second most common mistake runs the other way: scaling DSP spend on a rising impression count with no measurement plan behind it, because impressions are cheap to buy and easy to report. Impressions are not evidence. A rollback trigger set before spend goes up is what keeps that mistake from getting expensive — every change should carry the evidence behind it, a measurement plan, and a trigger to reverse it if the plan doesn't hold.

When the DSP Report Looks Bad, Check These Before You Cut Spend

  • Attribution window mismatch: DSP and Sponsored Ads default to different windows; comparing raw ROAS across them without normalizing the window is comparing two different questions.
  • Overlap with sponsored ads: if a shopper saw a DSP ad and clicked a Sponsored Product, both dashboards may claim the sale. AMC is the only place to see it wasn't double-counted.
  • Frequency with no cap: the same shoppers seeing the same creative dozens of times inflates spend without adding reach — check frequency before blaming the audience.
  • New-to-brand rate falling: if that share drops while ROAS holds, the campaign may be re-buying existing customers, not growing the file.
  • No holdout, ever: if the account has never run a matched control, nobody actually knows if the reported ROAS is real. That's the first gap to close, not the last.

Common questions

Is an "Amazon DSP business" the same as becoming an Amazon delivery driver or franchise owner?

No. That's Amazon's Delivery Service Partner program, a separate logistics business with vehicle leasing, driver payroll and its own capital requirements. This page is about Amazon DSP the ad platform. If you meant the delivery franchise, Amazon's own DSP partner page has the current figures.

Who can actually use Amazon DSP, the ad platform?

It's open to registered brands, vendors and agencies, either through a self-service console with an account minimum or through a managed service run by an agency or partner. Eligibility details shift, so check the current requirements in Amazon's ad console rather than relying on an older summary.

How is Amazon DSP priced?

DSP media is bought programmatically with Amazon's technology fee built into the media cost. Agencies running it on your behalf typically charge per seat, a flat management fee, or a percentage of managed spend. Ask any vendor to show both halves — the base fee and the percentage — since a hidden point of spend gets expensive fast at scale.

Does DSP replace Sponsored Products or Sponsored Display?

No. Sponsored ads catch demand that already exists — someone searching or browsing. DSP builds and retargets audiences before and after that search happens. Most accounts run both; DSP without any sponsored ads foundation usually underperforms because there's no bottom-of-funnel catch for the traffic it generates.

What's the minimum budget where Amazon DSP is worth running?

There's no single number, and structure matters more than a figure: you need enough spend and time to build a holdout group large enough to mean something, and enough volume to justify the AMC analysis overhead. Small accounts are often better served running sponsored ads alone until they clear that bar.

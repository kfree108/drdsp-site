The short answer

Amazon Flex, in plain terms

Amazon Flex is you, your own car, and Amazon's app. You sign in, claim available delivery "blocks" — set windows of time, usually a few hours — and get paid per block completed. No interview beyond a background check, no employees, no lease, no long-term commitment. You can stop tomorrow.

The trade-off is a hard ceiling. Your income is the block rate times how many blocks you can grab, and blocks are allocated by Amazon based on local demand, not on how badly you want hours. You cover your own gas, insurance, and vehicle wear out of that rate. There's no path from Flex driver to Flex "owner" — it's gig work, not a business you build equity in.

Amazon DSP: the delivery franchise, not the ad platform

A Delivery Service Partner is a business you own. You form a company, sign an agreement with Amazon, hire and manage drivers as your employees, and run a fleet of Amazon-branded vans that Amazon leases to you. Amazon assigns the routes; you're paid for routes completed, and out of that revenue you cover payroll, insurance, fuel, maintenance, and Amazon's own operational scorecards.

This is a real operating business with real liabilities, not a side hustle. Amazon publishes minimum capital contribution requirements and they change by market and over time — treat any specific number you read in a blog post as stale, including one you might see elsewhere, and check the current DSP portal for what's actually required before you commit any cash.

The trade-off, worked through

Say you work Flex blocks around a full-time job. Your income is block rate times blocks completed, minus mileage and wear on a car you already own and insure. There's no month where you owe Amazon money — worst case, blocks dry up and income goes to zero for that week.

Now run the same week as a DSP owner. You're not paid per delivery — you're paid per completed route, and every route has to cover a slice of driver payroll, van lease payments, fuel, and insurance before you see a dollar of profit. If a driver calls out sick, you either cover the route yourself or eat the shortfall. If fuel costs spike for a quarter, that hits your margin before it hits Amazon's. The DSP model has a real ceiling above Flex — you're running a fleet, not one car — but it comes with a floor of fixed costs that Flex never carries.

  • Flex: variable income, near-zero fixed cost, capped ceiling, exit anytime.
  • DSP: variable income against fixed cost, higher ceiling, multi-year commitment, real downside if volume drops.

The mistake people make moving from Flex to DSP

The most common one: treating the DSP program as "Flex, but bigger." It isn't. Flex is a driving job. A DSP is a small business with payroll, insurance renewals, and a P&L — the traits that make someone a reliable Flex driver (showing up, following the app, hitting delivery windows) are not the traits that make someone a solvent small-business owner. Hiring, scheduling, and handling driver turnover is the actual job once you own a DSP; driving is what your employees do.

The second mistake is underestimating the cash-flow gap. Payroll and lease payments start before your first route payment clears. People who expect DSP income to behave like Flex income — paid promptly per unit of work, no float required — run short on cash in the first quarter, not because the model is bad, but because they sized it like a gig instead of a business.

When the numbers don't work

If you're a Flex driver and blocks have dried up in your zone: check availability at off-peak hours and in adjacent zones before assuming demand is gone everywhere — it moves with local order volume, not evenly. Don't build a household budget on Flex as primary income in a thin market; it was never built to guarantee hours.

If you've applied to be a DSP owner and been rejected, or the market Amazon is offering looks saturated: talk to existing DSP owners operating in that market before you sign anything, and ask Amazon's DSP recruiting team directly about route density and how many DSPs already run there. A signed agreement is a multi-year commitment against real capital — the time to learn the market is thin is before you lease the first van, not after.

The other Amazon DSP

If what you actually meant by "DSP" is Amazon's advertising platform — the Demand-Side Platform used to buy display, video, and audio ads programmatically across and off Amazon — that's a different part of the company entirely. It lives inside Amazon Ads, not Amazon Logistics, and has nothing to do with vans, drivers, or delivery routes.

Which one you should actually pick

Flex suits someone who wants driving income with no capital and no commitment and can live with a hard ceiling. A Delivery Service Partner suits someone ready to run a small logistics business with real capital, payroll, and multi-year exposure for a higher ceiling. Neither is Amazon's advertising DSP, which is a media-buying platform for brands, not a job or a franchise.

Common questions

Can I drive for Flex and also own a DSP?

The DSP agreement includes exclusivity terms that may restrict outside driving work — check the current agreement rather than assume, since program terms change and we're not going to guess at legal language on your behalf.

Is a Delivery Service Partner a franchise?

Amazon doesn't officially call it a franchise, but it functions like one: branded vehicles, operational standards set by Amazon, and revenue tied to routes rather than to a fixed salary. You own the company and carry the liability.

Which pays more, Flex or a DSP?

It depends entirely on your market and how much capital and time you can commit. Flex is capped by block availability with almost no fixed cost. A DSP has a higher ceiling because you're running a fleet, but fixed costs — payroll, leases, insurance — come out before any profit does.

What is Amazon's other DSP, the advertising one?

Demand-Side Platform: Amazon's programmatic system for buying display, video, and audio ads. It's part of Amazon Ads, used by brands to run media campaigns, and has no connection to delivery vans, drivers, or the courier franchise program.

Do I need more than one van to run a DSP?

Yes — it's structured as a fleet business, not a single-vehicle operation. Amazon sets fleet size and route requirements as part of the program, and those requirements are published and updated by Amazon, so check the current DSP portal rather than a fixed number from an old post.

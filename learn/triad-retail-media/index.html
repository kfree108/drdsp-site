What Triad Retail Media actually was

Triad Retail Media was a shopper marketing agency founded in 2004 in St. Petersburg, Florida. It built and ran the media programs that let brands like Procter & Gamble and Unilever buy ads inside retailer sites and apps — starting with Walmart, then expanding to Sam's Club, eBay, CVS, Bed Bath & Beyond, Kohl's, Staples and Toys R Us.

Walmart was the business. Two former employees told Business Insider it made up 70% to 80% of Triad's revenue at its peak. WPP's media arm GroupM bought the firm in 2016 for $300 million. By April 2020 the company was gone — down from roughly 500 employees in 2017 to about 100 at the end.

Why it collapsed — the mechanism, not just the headline

The collapse had a mechanism, not just a headline. Triad sold Walmart's own audience back to Walmart's suppliers, using Walmart's data, on Walmart's site. That arrangement only works as long as Walmart needs an outside firm to run it. Once Walmart decided it could build the ad platform itself, keep the margin Triad used to take, and control the data end to end, the agency's reason to exist went with it.

Walmart formally cut ties in early 2019 and launched its own self-serve ad business in January 2020. Sam's Club, eBay and CVS followed the same pattern. A lawsuit alleging Triad's previous private-equity owners had inflated its EBITDA by more than 40% was still working through the courts when the shutdown happened — it didn't cause the collapse, but it shows the business was thinner than its $300 million price tag suggested.

COVID-19 gets named in the official statement. It mattered. But it was the push on a company that had already lost its anchor client and hadn't built a second one to replace it.

The gap Triad's model never closed: proof, not promise

Triad, like most shopper marketing agencies of that era, reported results the way most retail media still does today: last-click attribution. A shopper sees an ad, clicks it, buys something, and the sale gets credited to the ad. That number is real. It's not proof the ad caused the sale — the shopper might have bought anyway.

Holdout tests and matched controls answer the question last-click can't: what happened to a comparable group that didn't see the ad. Reconciling in Amazon Marketing Cloud matters for a related reason — DSP and sponsored ads both claim credit for the same shopper if you let them, and AMC is where that double-counting gets caught.

Here's what that looks like on a real book, not a hypothetical one: across 30 advertisers running Amazon DSP in July 2026, the managed campaigns delivered a 6.04x return on ad spend, 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM and a blended $1.42 cost-per-click. Blended cost per acquisition came to $5.49 across 57,137 attributed purchases, 20.1% of them from shoppers new to the brand. Every one of those numbers is still attribution — the same kind Triad reported. What sits behind them is different: a measurement plan and a rollback trigger agreed before the campaign ran, and a holdout group to check the attributed figure against.

What to do when the number comes back wrong

If a holdout test comes back flat — the exposed group and the control group buy at about the same rate — the honest move is not to hide it or rerun it until it looks better. It means the channel isn't adding incremental sales at that spend level, for that audience, right now. That can change with different creative, a different funnel stage, or a tighter audience. It doesn't change by re-attributing the same clicks a different way.

The mistake we've made, and seen made repeatedly across the group: crediting a DSP awareness campaign with sales that sponsored ads already claimed in last-click reporting. Two channels, one shopper, one sale, counted twice — the ROAS looks better on paper and worse in the bank account. Every change should carry three things before it runs: the evidence behind it, a measurement plan, and a rollback trigger. Reconciling in AMC before the budget conversation, not after, is the fix.

Why 'Triad Retail Media' shows up in searches today

People searching this term are usually looking for one of three things: a company to hire, a historical footnote, or confirmation that something no longer exists under that name. The agency is gone. Its technology and engineering team were sold to Sam's Club in 2019, ahead of the wider shutdown. There's no Triad Retail Media platform to sign up for now.

What replaced it is the pattern that killed it: retailers running their own ad platforms — Walmart Connect, Amazon's advertising business, Target's Roundel — alongside managed partners who run programmatic media inside those platforms on a brand's behalf. That's a different business model than a shopper marketing agency, even where the org chart looks similar on paper.

Common questions

Is Triad Retail Media still in business?

No. GroupM, WPP's media-buying arm, shut the agency down in April 2020 after Walmart, its largest client by far, moved its advertising business in-house. There is no Triad Retail Media platform operating today.

Who owned Triad Retail Media?

It was founded in 2004 and later owned by Rockbridge Growth Equity and Falcon Investment Advisors before WPP's GroupM acquired it in 2016 for $300 million. A lawsuit later alleged the prior owners had overstated its earnings before the sale.

What replaced shopper marketing agencies like Triad?

Two things, mostly. Retailers built their own in-house ad platforms — Walmart Connect being the direct successor to Triad's old business — and brands started hiring managed partners to run programmatic buying like Amazon DSP on their behalf, rather than a full-service shopper marketing agency.

Was COVID-19 the real reason Triad shut down?

It was named in the official statement, but former employees pointed to earlier causes: the loss of Walmart's contract in 2019, similar losses at Sam's Club, eBay and CVS, and a failure to diversify the business fast enough. COVID accelerated a decision that was largely already made.

How is DSP measurement today different from what Triad reported?

Triad-era shopper marketing reported last-click attribution — a real number, but not proof an ad caused a sale. Holdout tests and matched controls, reconciled in Amazon Marketing Cloud to remove double-counting between DSP and sponsored ads, are what actually establish incrementality.

Two different "DSPs" — and this page is about the ad platform

Search this phrase and you'll get results about Amazon's Delivery Service Partner program — the franchise where small-business owners own delivery vans and employ drivers. That's a real program, and a good one, but it has nothing to do with advertising.

This page is about Amazon DSP, the Demand-Side Platform: the system that buys display, video, and audio ads programmatically across Amazon and off-Amazon inventory. "Ownership" in this context has nothing to do with vans or franchises. It means who holds the advertiser ID in Amazon's ad console, who controls the seat, and who keeps the audience segments, pixels, and log-level data if the relationship with an agency or vendor ends.

How Amazon DSP seat ownership actually works

Every Amazon DSP advertiser sits under a seat — a container that has billing terms, user permissions, and access to Amazon Marketing Cloud (AMC). Seats come in three practical flavors:

  • Brand-owned seat: the brand's own entity is the named advertiser. An agency can be granted user access, but the brand controls the seat and can revoke access at any time.
  • Agency-owned or trading-desk seat: the agency's entity is the named advertiser, and the brand is a sub-account or line item underneath it. The agency controls billing, permissions, and — critically — the AMC instance.
  • Hybrid/co-managed seat: a brand-owned seat where a vendor is granted advanced permissions to run day-to-day operations, but admin rights and data exports stay with the brand.

The seat type decides who can pull raw, unaggregated event-level data out of AMC, who owns the pixel firing on the brand's own site, and who can take the built audience segments to a different vendor. A contract can promise "you own your data," but if the underlying seat is agency-owned, that promise depends entirely on the agency's willingness to export it — there's no technical mechanism forcing it.

A worked example: what changes when you move from an agency-owned seat to a brand-owned one

Full Circle and reMKTR currently operate 109 live Amazon DSP advertiser seats across the group. In practice, most of those started as agency-owned — the fastest way to get a new advertiser live — and a portion have since migrated to brand-owned seats as clients grew or needed direct control for compliance, M&A, or multi-vendor reasons.

The mechanics of that migration matter more than the paperwork. Moving a seat means: re-pointing the AMC instance to the brand's own AWS account, re-tagging pixels so first-party data flows to the brand's own systems rather than the agency's, and re-negotiating the historical audience segments — some carry over cleanly, some don't because they were built on agency-side identifiers. None of that happens automatically. It has to be planned, tested, and verified before the old seat is closed, or the brand loses months of audience history overnight.

When the answer is bad news: your seat isn't really yours

The most common discovery is a brand finding out, mid-negotiation with a new vendor, that they don't actually hold the advertiser ID — the incumbent agency does. That's not always malicious; it's often just how the seat was set up years earlier when nobody asked the question. But it means the brand cannot simply "switch vendors" — it has to migrate.

If you're in that position, here's the sequence that actually works: request a written confirmation of seat type and advertiser ID ownership first, before terminating anything. Then request an AMC export of the last 12 months of instance data — this is the piece most often forgotten, and once a seat is closed, historical log-level data can become unrecoverable. Only then negotiate an exit timeline that overlaps old and new seats long enough to rebuild audiences with a measurement plan, not a guess.

The mistakes we see most — including ones we've made

The biggest mistake is treating seat ownership as a legal question rather than an operational one. A contract clause saying "client owns all data" is worth little if nobody actually tests the export process until the day the relationship ends. We've had to rebuild audience segments from scratch on a migration where the historical identifiers didn't map cleanly — a problem a pre-migration test would have caught.

The second mistake is confusing seat ownership with attribution ownership. Even a fully brand-owned DSP seat doesn't answer whether the display spend running through it actually did anything — last-click attribution inside the DSP console can't prove incrementality, because it can't show what would have happened without the ad. That requires holdouts or matched controls, reconciled against sponsored ads spend in AMC so DSP and search aren't double-counting the same purchase. Owning the seat and proving the spend worked are two separate problems, and most disputes we see conflate them.

Common questions

Is Amazon DSP the same as Amazon's Delivery Service Partner program?

No. Amazon DSP is the Demand-Side Platform for buying programmatic display, video, and audio ads. Delivery Service Partner is an unrelated small-business franchise program for last-mile package delivery. Same three letters, completely different businesses.

Who owns the data collected in Amazon DSP campaigns?

It depends on the seat type. On a brand-owned seat, the brand owns the advertiser ID and, by extension, controls the AMC instance and exportable data. On an agency-owned seat, the agency holds those rights and grants access — ownership in practice depends on the contract and the agency's cooperation at exit.

Can I move my Amazon DSP seat from one agency to another?

Yes, but it's a migration, not a switch. It involves re-pointing the AMC instance, re-tagging pixels, and rebuilding or transferring audience segments. Plan an overlap period between old and new vendors so nothing lapses mid-migration.

Does a brand-owned seat cost more than an agency-managed one?

Not inherently — the seat type doesn't set the price. Fees are usually structured as a percentage of spend, a flat retainer, or a hybrid, and that structure is set by whoever manages the seat, not by who legally owns it. Ask any vendor to state the structure plainly rather than assuming brand-owned means cheaper.

What happens to my audiences and pixels if I switch DSP vendors?

If the seat is brand-owned, audiences and pixel data generally transfer with minimal disruption. If it's agency-owned, some identifiers and historical segments may not map cleanly to a new environment, and a portion of audience history can be lost unless the migration is tested before the old seat closes.

What programmatic DOOH actually is

Programmatic DOOH takes a physical screen — a digital billboard, a transit shelter panel, a screen at a gas pump — and sells the ad space on it the way a website sells a banner: through software, in real time or on automated terms, instead of a media buyer calling the screen owner and signing an insertion order. The advertiser sets rules (budget, screen type, time window, sometimes a weather or event trigger) and a bidding platform decides which screens run the ad and when.

One clarification before we go further: "DSP" in this space means demand-side platform, the software that does the bidding — the same term Amazon uses for Amazon DSP. Worth stating plainly: Amazon DSP transacts display, video and audio inventory, on Amazon and off it. It is not one of the platforms bidding into street-level screen networks. If the plan is to buy actual billboards and transit screens, that's a different set of platforms than the ones this page's publisher runs.

The mechanics: RTB, PMP, and programmatic guaranteed

Screens get sold programmatically in three ways, and they trade off speed against certainty:

  • Open exchange RTB — a live auction. Fastest to activate, cheapest on average, least control over exactly which screen wins.
  • Private marketplace (PMP) deals — a negotiated floor price and reserved access to a specific pool of screens. More control, less spontaneity.
  • Programmatic guaranteed / automated direct — a fixed impression commitment with the paperwork automated, no live auction. Best when a launch date can't slip.

None of these change what's on the screen. They change how the slot got sold, which is the entire point of the word "programmatic" here — it describes the transaction, not the medium.

A worked example: what the budget actually buys

Here's the arithmetic that most explainers skip. Say a network prices its screens at a $12 CPM and the budget is $15,000. That's roughly 1.25 million impressions ($15,000 ÷ $12 × 1,000). But an "impression" on a shared screen is a play, not a confirmed set of eyeballs — it's the number of times the creative ran while the network's audience model says people were plausibly nearby.

If the network also reports frequency — say each unique passerby is modeled to see the screen twice on average during the flight — the effective reach for that budget is closer to 625,000 people, not 1.25 million. Screens report the ceiling. The real number is always lower, and any plan that treats screen plays as a headcount is already off by whatever the frequency multiple turns out to be.

The common mistake — including one we've made ourselves

The mistake shows up when a geofenced visit or a device ID passback near the screen gets credited like a last-click sale. A shared screen has no single identity behind each exposure. Someone counted as a "visit" after seeing the ad might have already been walking to that store — the exposure and the outcome look identical whether the ad caused anything or not.

We've run this exact mistake down on our own book, on the online side. Last-click and view-through attribution told us campaigns were working when they were often crediting demand that already existed. Across 30 of those advertisers in July 2026, the book delivered 6.04x return on ad spend, 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM and a blended $1.42 cost-per-click — numbers that only mean something once checked against a holdout, because raw attribution tells a nearly identical story whether the media caused the sale or just happened to be nearby when it was going to happen anyway.

When the number is bad news: what to check before touching creative

Work down this order before assuming the campaign failed:

  • Definition mismatch first. "Visit" might mean a 30-second dwell on one network's panel and a 5-minute dwell on another's. Compare definitions before comparing numbers.
  • Dedupe second. Check whether the platform is counting the same commuter twice across two screens on the same route.
  • Sample size third. Foot-traffic panels thin out fast outside major metros — a bad number in a small market can just be a small sample.
  • Creative and targeting last, only once the first three are ruled out.

If none of that explains it, the fix isn't "run it longer." It's writing down what would count as proof the change worked, and what triggers pulling it back, before the next flight starts — not after the invoice arrives.

Common questions

Is programmatic DOOH the same as digital out-of-home (DOOH)?

No. DOOH just means the screen is digital rather than static vinyl. Programmatic DOOH describes how that digital screen gets sold — automated bidding or automated deal paperwork instead of a phone call and an insertion order. A brand can run digital screens for years without ever buying them programmatically.

Does Amazon DSP buy programmatic DOOH inventory?

Not directly. Amazon DSP transacts display, video and audio inventory, on Amazon and off it, but it isn't one of the demand-side platforms bidding into street-level screen networks. If the plan calls for billboards, transit panels or retail screens, that's OOH-specific DSPs, not Amazon DSP.

How is pDOOH measured without cookies or device IDs?

Through aggregated signals instead of individual ones: geofenced mobile panels, in-screen sensors, camera-based audience counts, and sometimes device ID passback where regulations allow it. All of these estimate exposure at the screen level and infer downstream behavior from a sample — none match the person-level cleanliness of web analytics.

What's the difference between RTB, PMP, and programmatic guaranteed for OOH?

RTB is a live auction — fastest and cheapest, least control over which exact screen wins. PMP is a negotiated deal with a floor price and a reserved pool of screens. Programmatic guaranteed is a fixed impression commitment with the paperwork automated but no live bidding — the pick for a launch date that can't move.

Can you retarget someone after they see a pDOOH ad?

Only indirectly, through geofenced mobile retargeting tied to proximity to the screen — not through a cookie or pixel on the ad itself, since there's no browser involved. It's an inference based on location, not a one-to-one match to the exposure.

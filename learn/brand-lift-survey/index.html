The short answer

A brand lift survey measures whether an ad campaign shifted opinion — awareness, consideration, purchase intent — by comparing survey answers from people exposed to the ad against a matched control group who weren't. Google, YouTube, and Meta each run their own version. None of them measure sales.

What a Brand Lift Survey Actually Measures

A brand lift survey measures whether an ad campaign changed what people think about a brand — not whether it changed what they bought. Google and YouTube run their own version inside Google Ads. Meta runs its own inside Ads Manager. Both compare survey answers from people who were exposed to the ad against a matched group who weren't.

The metrics are all perception, not transaction: ad recall, brand awareness, consideration, purchase intent, brand association. Google's own documentation is explicit about this — surveys go to two groups, people who saw the ad and people who were eligible to see it but didn't because it wasn't shown. The gap between how those two groups answer is the lift.

That's a genuinely useful thing to measure. It's also narrower than most people assume when they hear the word lift. A brand lift survey tells you whether an ad moved a stated opinion in a survey response. It can't tell you whether the campaign paid for itself, because it was never built to touch a register.

How It Actually Works: Exposed vs. Control

The mechanics are close to identical across YouTube, Google's Demand Gen surveys, and Meta's Brand Lift product, even though the interfaces look different:

  • The platform holds the ad back from a randomly selected slice of your target audience — the control group.
  • Everyone else in the study — the exposed group — sees the ad as normal.
  • A short survey (often one question) gets served to both groups at a natural break point: before a YouTube video, in a Meta feed, as a Google survey unit.
  • The platform compares the positive response rate between the two groups. That gap is the absolute brand lift.

Two things decide whether the gap means anything: sample size and spend. Google flags this directly in its own help docs — a study can sit at 'Not Eligible' for days if the campaign hasn't cleared the minimum spend threshold, and hitting 100% progress before the measurement window closes doesn't mean you can pull budget early. The study needs volume to reach a stable read, not just a directionally positive one.

A Worked Example: Turning Survey Answers Into a Number

Here's the arithmetic behind the headline number, using round figures to show the mechanic rather than any single campaign's real result. Say 400,000 people are exposed to a campaign and 400,000 are held out as control. In the survey, 22% of the exposed group gives a positive answer against 16% of the control group. The absolute brand lift is 6 percentage points. If the platform attributes 40,000 lifted users to that gap, and the campaign spent $80,000, cost per lifted user is $2.00. That's the entire calculation — no more, no less.

What that number can't tell you is whether any of those 40,000 people bought anything. That's a different measurement problem, and it's the one we spend most of our time on. On Amazon specifically, the honest way to answer it is a holdout or matched-market test reconciled in Amazon Marketing Cloud, where DSP exposure and sponsored-ads exposure stop double-counting each other. Across 30 of our advertisers in July 2026, that kind of measurement — not survey lift — is what showed a 6.04x return on ad spend across 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM. That's a sales number, arrived at without asking anyone a survey question.

Where Brand Lift Surveys Break Down

Three limits show up in every study, regardless of platform:

  • Eligibility gating. Google's own troubleshooting docs describe studies stuck at 'Not Eligible' even after the advertiser fixes the underlying issue, because the status updates on a delay. Managing budget against a status that's still catching up means managing against a number that's already stale.
  • Low-response noise. A 3- or 4-point gap between exposed and control groups can sit inside margin of error on a modest sample. The platform will still render a positive lift because the math produces a number. The number isn't automatically meaningful.
  • No path to sales. Brand lift surveys sit entirely upper-funnel. A brand like Beardbrand or Epic Gardening running video can get a real signal that recall moved and still have no idea whether that translated into a single incremental purchase, because the survey was never connected to a transaction.

None of this makes the tools useless. It means a brand lift survey answers 'did perception move,' and a holdout test answers 'did sales move because of the ad.' They aren't substitutes for each other, and treating one as proof of the other is the actual mistake.

The Common Mistake — Including One We've Made

The mistake we see most, and one we've made ourselves, is reading a positive score from a modest-spend YouTube study and treating it as evidence the campaign earned its budget back. It's an easy leap because the platform hands you a clean-looking percentage. It's also the wrong leap: a six-point survey lift says stated recall moved, not that revenue moved, and definitely not that revenue moved enough to cover the media bill.

The fix isn't to distrust the survey. It's to pair it with a measurement built to answer the sales question before deciding whether to scale spend. On Amazon, that means running a holdout or matched-market test and reconciling it in Amazon Marketing Cloud, where display exposure and sponsored ads exposure get separated out instead of both claiming credit for the same purchase. Every change we make carries three things before it runs: the evidence behind it, a measurement plan, and a rollback trigger — because 'the survey number went up' is not, on its own, evidence.

Which one you should actually pick

Native brand lift surveys on Google, YouTube, and Meta suit anyone who wants a fast, free read on whether upper-funnel messaging is landing, and who accepts the answer stops at perception. Holdout and matched-market tests suit anyone who needs to know if spend produced sales — the question a survey was never built to answer.

Common questions

Is a brand lift survey the same as a holdout test?

No. A brand lift survey compares stated opinions between an exposed group and a control group. A holdout test compares actual purchase behavior between the same kinds of groups. They answer different questions and are often run alongside each other, not instead of each other.

How long does a Google or YouTube Brand Lift study take to run?

Google requires spend to clear a minimum threshold within a set measurement window before a study collects enough responses to report reliably. Pulling budget early because the progress indicator looks close to done can leave the study underpowered and the result unreliable.

Can you run a brand lift survey on Amazon?

Amazon doesn't offer a native Brand Lift survey product the way YouTube or Meta does. Advertisers measuring Amazon DSP impact typically use holdout or matched-market tests reconciled in Amazon Marketing Cloud instead, which measures actual purchase behavior rather than survey responses.

Does a positive brand lift score mean sales went up?

Not on its own. It means the exposed group answered a perception question more positively than the control group. Whether that shift produced incremental sales is a separate measurement question a survey alone can't answer.

What's a good sample size for a brand lift study?

There's no single number — it depends on the baseline response rate and the size of the gap you're trying to detect. This is exactly why platforms gate studies behind minimum spend and impression thresholds: below a certain volume, the exposed-versus-control gap is just noise.

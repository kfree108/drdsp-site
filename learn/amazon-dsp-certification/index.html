What the certification actually is

Amazon DSP certification lives inside Amazon Ads Academy, alongside certifications for sponsored ads and other Amazon Ads products. It's free. You pick the DSP learning path, work through the course content at your own pace, then sit a graded assessment. Pass it and a digital badge lands in your account within 24 hours, with a downloadable PDF certificate you can share on LinkedIn.

Worth saying plainly, because the search term is genuinely ambiguous: this is Amazon DSP, the Demand-Side Platform for buying display, video and audio inventory programmatically. It has nothing to do with Amazon's Delivery Service Partner program, the courier franchise business. If you searched for one and landed on information about the other, this is the advertising one.

The assessment has to be finished in one sitting — 60 to 90 minutes, no saving progress if you exit or lose connection. Fail it and you wait 24 hours before retaking, with no cap on attempts. Certifications expire, and recertifying means redoing the course modules, not just the test again.

How to get certified, step by step

  • Create an Amazon Ads Academy account — free, open to anyone, not just people with an active DSP seat.
  • Find the DSP certification learning path in the catalog and work through the required modules. This is where the actual product knowledge gets built — audience types, deal structures, measurement tools inside the console.
  • Block 60–90 uninterrupted minutes for the assessment itself. Stable connection matters; a dropped session means starting over.
  • Pass, and the badge appears within 24 hours. Download the certificate, add it to LinkedIn if you want the credential visible externally.
  • If you fail, wait 24 hours and retake. There's no limit on attempts, so it's a knowledge gap to close, not a one-shot exam.

None of this requires an active DSP account or live spend. That's useful for anyone building skills before a role exists, and it's also the first thing worth knowing about what the credential does and doesn't verify.

What the badge proves — and the gap it leaves

The assessment tests whether you know how the console works: how audiences are built, what deal types exist, which measurement products Amazon offers, how to structure a line item. That's real, useful knowledge. It is not a test of whether your campaigns are working.

Here's the gap, stated concretely. Full Circle and reMKTR operate 109 live Amazon DSP advertiser seats. Across 30 of those advertisers in July 2026, the book delivered 6.04x return on ad spend, 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM and a blended $1.42 cost-per-click, with a blended $5.49 cost per acquisition across 57,137 attributed purchases, 20.1% of them new-to-brand. None of those numbers come from a certification exam. They come from holdout tests and matched controls reconciled against sponsored ads in Amazon Marketing Cloud — the only way to know whether DSP added incremental sales instead of just showing up last in a path a shopper was already on.

The certification doesn't cover incrementality measurement, holdout design, or AMC reconciliation, because those aren't console features — they're practices layered on top. A certified analyst can build a technically correct campaign that's still being judged by last-click attribution, which cannot prove incrementality and never could.

The common mistake — including one worth admitting

The mistake we see most, and one that's easy to make even with a fully certified team: treating a passing badge as proof the DSP program is working. It proves the operator knows where the buttons are. It says nothing about whether the spend is incremental.

If your team is certified and DSP performance still looks flat or the ROAS reported in-platform doesn't match what finance sees, recertifying won't fix it. The fix is checking the measurement setup: is attribution last-click only, is there a holdout or matched-control test running, is DSP being reconciled against sponsored ads in AMC so the two channels stop double-counting the same conversion. Every change to that setup should carry the evidence behind it, a measurement plan, and a rollback trigger — that discipline lives outside the certification entirely, and it's the part that actually determines whether the program is worth the spend.

Who needs this certification, and who doesn't

Self-service DSP operators, agency media buyers, and anyone hands-on in the console benefit from getting certified — it shortens the learning curve on mechanics and gives hiring managers a baseline signal. Brands that outsource DSP to a managed-service provider don't need the badge themselves, but understanding what it does and doesn't cover is useful when evaluating that provider's team: ask about their measurement practice, not their badge count.

Common questions

Do I need Amazon DSP certification to actually buy on the platform?

No. Certification is a knowledge credential, not an account requirement. You can run DSP campaigns without it, and plenty of certified people have never touched a live seat.

Does the certification cover incrementality or holdout testing?

No. It covers console mechanics — audience building, deal structures, available measurement tools. Holdout design and AMC reconciliation are operating practices that sit on top of the platform, not exam content.

How long is the certification valid before I need to recertify?

Certifications expire and recertifying requires redoing the required course modules, not just retaking the assessment. Check your My Achievements page for your specific expiration date.

Is Amazon DSP certification the same thing as Amazon's Delivery Service Partner program?

No, and the names collide often in search. DSP certification here is about the advertising Demand-Side Platform — programmatic display, video and audio buying. Delivery Service Partner is Amazon's separate courier franchise business.

Should I use certification badges to evaluate an agency's DSP team?

It's a reasonable baseline signal for console literacy, but it doesn't tell you how that team measures performance. Ask separately how they handle attribution, holdout tests, and reconciliation against sponsored ads — that's where the real difference between teams shows up.

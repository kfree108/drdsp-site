The short answer

Criteo retail media is Criteo's ad-tech business: it helps retailers sell ad space on their own sites and helps brands buy that space across hundreds of retailers. It has no role in Amazon's own retail media — Sponsored Products and Amazon DSP (demand-side platform, not the delivery courier) run entirely on Amazon's stack.

What Criteo's retail media platform actually does

Criteo is not a retailer. It's an ad-tech company that sits on both sides of retail media: Commerce Yield gives retailers the plumbing to sell ad space on their own sites and apps, and Commerce Max and Commerce Grid let brands and agencies buy that space — plus off-retailer inventory across the open internet — through one seat. Commerce Growth and Criteo GO sit further upstream, running acquisition and retention media that isn't tied to any retailer's site at all.

Underneath all five products is the same data layer: a shopper graph linking browsing and purchase signals across the retailers Criteo works with, and a predictive bidding model that scores each impression by the likelihood that specific shopper converts. Criteo states it works with more than 4,000 brands and 200+ retailers globally — that scale is the actual product. A single brand buy can appear across dozens of retailer sites without the brand negotiating each one separately.

What Criteo does not do is run Amazon's own advertising stack. More on that below, because it's the single most common reason someone searching "criteo retail media" ends up on the wrong page.

How a retail media buy actually works, step by step

Strip away the branding and every retail media buy — Criteo's, Amazon's, anyone's — follows the same five steps: the retailer opens up ad inventory on search results, category pages or app; the platform scores each shopper against a bid; an auction clears at a CPM or CPC; the ad serves next to or inside the shopping experience; and the retailer hands the brand a performance report, almost always built on last-click attribution inside a fixed look-back window.

To make the math concrete: across 30 advertisers we managed on Amazon DSP in July 2026 — a different network, not Criteo's, but the same mechanics — the book ran 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM with a blended $1.42 CPC, and returned 6.04x ROAS. That's the shape of every retail media report you'll see: an impression price, a click price, and a return ratio quoted as if it settles the argument. It doesn't, and that's the part every vendor homepage skips.

That same book carried a blended $5.49 cost per acquisition across 57,137 attributed purchases, 20.1% of them new-to-brand. Numbers like that look precise. They are precise — about what the platform decided to credit itself with. Whether the sale would have happened anyway is a separate question, and last-click attribution cannot answer it.

Criteo retail media vs. Amazon retail media — don't confuse them

If you sell primarily on Amazon and searched "criteo retail media" hoping to find your Sponsored Products dashboard, you're in the wrong system. Amazon runs Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, Sponsored Display and Amazon DSP entirely on its own stack inside Amazon Ads. Criteo has no access to that auction and no seat inside it — its retail media network is built with hundreds of other retailers, not Amazon.

A kitchen brand like HexClad or a beauty brand like BK Beauty selling mainly through Amazon's own marketplace needs Amazon's ad console — managed directly or through a partner — not Criteo's Commerce Max. Where Criteo genuinely fits is a brand selling across a wide retailer footprint: grocery chains, department stores, marketplaces outside Amazon, where buying media retailer-by-retailer would mean twenty separate negotiations instead of one.

The two systems can sit side by side in a brand's media plan. They are never the same system, and no page that treats "retail media" as one undifferentiated thing is being straight with you.

Why the ROAS number on the dashboard can be lying to you

Every retail media network — Criteo's included — reports sales inside a click or view window and calls it attributed revenue. That number answers "did a sale happen after this ad ran," not "did this ad cause the sale." Someone who already had the product in their cart, or who searches your brand name out of habit, gets counted the same way as someone who saw the ad and bought for the first time.

We've made this mistake ourselves: crediting display media for sales that were really coming from brand search or a promotion running at the same time. The fix isn't a better attribution model — it's a holdout. Hold back a matched group of shoppers from the campaign, run the media against everyone else, and compare what actually happened between the two groups. That's the only honest way to separate "this ad worked" from "this sale would have happened anyway."

The 20.1% new-to-brand figure above is closer to that honest answer than raw ROAS, because it at least segments by whether the buyer had a prior relationship with the brand. It's still not a holdout. Treat any retail media report — from Criteo, from Amazon, from anyone — as a starting hypothesis, not a verdict.

What to do when the number looks wrong

Three things go wrong on repeat, across every retail media network: the attribution window changed without anyone noticing, a setting that was supposed to switch off is still running, or last week's "fix" made no measurable difference at all.

  • The number looks wrong: check the look-back window and attribution model before anything else — a shift from 14-day to 7-day click attribution alone can move ROAS by a wide margin with zero change in actual sales.
  • The setting is already on: pull the live campaign settings, not the plan from the kickoff call. Budget caps, dayparting and audience exclusions drift after launch and rarely get reverted.
  • The fix didn't work: if a change ran for a full cycle and the holdout group performed the same as the exposed group, the honest read is that the media added nothing measurable — not that the test was flawed.

None of this is unique to Criteo. It's the standard failure mode of any platform that reports its own performance back to you.

Common questions

Does Criteo run ads on Amazon?

No. Criteo's retail media network is built with hundreds of other retailers — it has no access to Amazon's advertising auction. Amazon's Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, Sponsored Display and Amazon DSP all run inside Amazon's own ad console, independent of Criteo.

What's the difference between Criteo GO and Commerce Max?

Criteo GO is self-serve performance advertising for brands running their own campaigns. Commerce Max is aimed at brands and agencies buying retail media inventory across the retailer networks Criteo connects to, typically at higher spend and with more account support.

How much does Criteo retail media cost?

Criteo doesn't publish retail media pricing on its own site. Contact their sales team for a quote rather than trusting a third-party number, since pricing structures for platforms like this vary by scope and spend and change without notice.

Can a brand use Criteo and Amazon DSP at the same time?

Yes, and many do. Criteo covers retail media on non-Amazon retailers plus open-web performance media; Amazon DSP covers display, video and audio inside Amazon. They're complementary media plans, not competing versions of the same channel, though the two rarely reconcile their reporting with each other automatically.

Is Criteo's ROAS number trustworthy?

It's accurate as a report of sales inside its attribution window — it's not proof the media caused those sales. Like any retail media network's dashboard, it needs a holdout or matched-control test to separate incremental sales from sales that would have happened anyway.

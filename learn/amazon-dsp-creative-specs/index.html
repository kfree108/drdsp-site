The short answer

Amazon DSP display creative accepts JPEG, PNG, GIF, and HTML5, in standard IAB sizes from 300x50 up to 970x250, with file-size caps by size that range from 60 KB to 150 KB. Video runs as MP4, 16:9, up to 500 MB. Streaming TV has its own, stricter set of requirements layered on top of the standard video specs.

Display: the sizes and file-size caps that actually matter

Amazon's own eCommerce display creative (REC) documentation supports both a responsive option — a single asset that Amazon automatically resizes to fit whatever breakpoints a placement needs — and a fixed set of specific IAB sizes for advertisers who want direct control: 300x250, 336x280, 728x90, 160x600, 300x600, 320x50, 300x50, 414x125, 970x250, and 980x55.

File-size limits are set per size, not applied as one blanket number, and this is the part most creative teams get wrong on the first upload: standard sizes cap at 100 KB, the 728x90 leaderboard caps lower at 60 KB, mobile sizes (300x50, 320x50, 414x125) also cap at 60 KB, and the large formats (970x250, 980x55) get more room at 150 KB. Accepted formats are JPEG, PNG, GIF and HTML — Amazon's spec table lists HTML alongside the static formats under the same per-size weight caps. An HTML unit keeps loading assets after the ad first renders, so its initial-load weight and its total weight are not the same number; Amazon does not publish a separate initial-load ceiling on that page, so check what the console accepts on upload rather than budgeting against a figure from a spec blog.

The responsive option is worth defaulting to for most brands, specifically because it removes the size-by-size guesswork this section describes. A single well-built asset, submitted once, gets automatically resized and re-compressed to fit whatever breakpoint a given placement calls for — trading some pixel-level control for a large reduction in the number of individual files a creative team has to build, test, and re-upload every time a new size gets added to a campaign.

A worked example: sizing a creative for the leaderboard slot

Say a designer builds a 728x90 leaderboard at 1x resolution, exports it as a PNG, and it comes out at 84 KB. That file will be rejected on upload — it's under the 100 KB standard cap but over the 60 KB cap that applies specifically to that size. The fix is almost never "compress harder" as a first move; it's checking which size-specific cap actually applies before building the asset, because a JPEG re-export at 60% quality to hit 60 KB on a leaderboard will visibly degrade the creative, while the same 60 KB budget is comfortable for a smaller mobile unit with less surface area to render.

The practical order to work in: confirm the exact pixel dimensions and which file-size tier they fall under first, design and export against that number second, and only reach for aggressive compression as a last resort — most rejected uploads in practice are sized correctly but built against the wrong cap for that dimension.

Video and streaming TV: two specs, not one

Amazon DSP video runs as MP4, 16:9 aspect ratio, up to 500 MB, and — per Amazon's own documentation — is currently available in the U.S. only on third-party publisher sites for the standard online-video placement. That's a materially different, stricter product from streaming TV inventory, which runs on connected-TV and OTT surfaces and layers its own requirements on top: audio levels have to meet broadcast loudness standards, not just "loud enough," and duration has to land on one of the lengths the supply source actually accepts rather than anywhere near it. Amazon's own streaming TV spec table publishes those lengths per supply source — in the US, 15, 30, 45 and 60 seconds on Prime Video; 15 and 30 on Twitch; 15, 30, 45, 60 and 90 on Fire TV Channels; and any duration at the publisher's discretion on third-party exchange inventory. The lists differ by locale, so read the row for the market you are buying. A :31 cut that would pass on an online-video placement gets rejected outright on streaming TV.

This split matters because it's the same mistake as confusing display formats: a video asset built for a phone screen inside an app, with fast cuts and on-screen captions because sound is often off, is not automatically fit for a connected-TV living-room screen, where sound is almost always on and the viewer isn't holding the device. Building one video and dropping it into both placement types without adjusting for the viewing context is the single most common creative mistake we see cross the desk, on our own accounts included.

When a creative gets rejected: what to check, in order

A rejected creative is not a mystery if you work through it in the right order. Check the file size against the size-specific cap first, not the general 100 KB standard — this catches most rejections on the first pass. Check the exact pixel dimensions second; a creative built at 729x91 instead of 728x90 fails even if every other spec is correct. For HTML, check the initial-load weight separately from total weight third, because a unit that looks fine as a total download can still render too heavy on first paint. For video, check duration to the frame and audio loudness fourth, especially on anything headed to streaming TV, where both are enforced more strictly than on standard online video.

Which one you should actually pick

Creative teams building assets in-house benefit most from Amazon's own responsive REC option, which removes the size-by-size guesswork entirely. Teams that need pixel-level control over a specific placement — or are repurposing existing brand creative — need to check the size-specific file cap and, for video, the online-video-versus-streaming-TV split, before a single asset gets built, not after it's rejected.

Common questions

What file formats does Amazon DSP accept for display ads?

JPEG, PNG, GIF and HTML. Amazon's spec table lists HTML under the same per-size weight caps as the static formats; it does not publish a separate initial-load ceiling for HTML units, so confirm what the console accepts rather than budgeting against a third-party spec sheet.

Why does Amazon DSP have different file-size limits for different ad sizes?

Because file-size budget is set per placement, not as one blanket rule. Standard sizes cap at 100 KB, the 728x90 leaderboard and mobile sizes cap lower at 60 KB, and the large formats (970x250, 980x55) get more room at 150 KB. Building against the wrong tier for a given size is the most common cause of a rejected upload.

Can I use the same video creative for online video and streaming TV on Amazon DSP?

Not without checking the spec first. Streaming TV enforces stricter requirements than standard online video — duration must land on one of the exact lengths Amazon publishes for that supply source and locale (in the US: 15/30/45/60 on Prime Video, 15/30 on Twitch, 15/30/45/60/90 on Fire TV Channels), and audio must meet broadcast loudness standards. A cut built for a mobile app placement often fails those checks even when the file format and aspect ratio are otherwise correct.

What's the most common reason an Amazon DSP creative gets rejected?

Applying the general 100 KB display cap to a size that actually has a lower, size-specific limit — most often the 728x90 leaderboard or a mobile unit, both capped at 60 KB. Checking the exact tier for the exact dimensions before exporting the file avoids most rejections.

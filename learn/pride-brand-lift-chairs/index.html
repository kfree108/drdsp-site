Two searches, one string of words

If you typed this looking for a recliner that helps someone stand up safely, Pride Mobility's own site, My Mobility Store, and US Medical Supplies all sell the actual product — models, sizing, heat and massage options, and in some cases FDA Class II documentation for insurance reimbursement. That's a real, specific need and those retailers are built for it. We are not a furniture company and won't pretend otherwise.

The other reading of this phrase is brand lift — an advertising term that has nothing to do with recliners. It's what's left when you strip "Pride" and "chairs" out and ask: did this campaign actually change how people think about the brand, or just reach people who already intended to buy? That's the question this page answers.

What brand lift actually measures

Brand lift is the difference between what happened to people who saw an ad and what would have happened to those same people anyway. Last-click attribution can't answer that — it just credits whoever clicked last, whether or not the ad caused anything. A holdout group, or a matched control group with the same purchase history and demographics but no exposure, can.

Run the campaign against the exposed group, withhold it from the holdout, then compare outcomes — new-to-brand purchases, search behavior, repeat rate — between the two. Where it gets messy is when DSP and sponsored ads both touch the same shopper; without reconciling in Amazon Marketing Cloud, both channels claim the same sale and the lift number is fiction. AMC is the only place that separates the two honestly.

A worked example, with real numbers

Across 30 advertisers we managed in July 2026, the book delivered a 6.04x return on ad spend, 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM, and a blended $1.42 cost-per-click. On its own that tells you spend efficiency, not incrementality.

The number that actually speaks to lift is this: blended cost per acquisition was $5.49 across 57,137 attributed purchases, and 20.1% of those purchases came from shoppers new to the brand. That new-to-brand share is the tell. If DSP were purely retargeting people who were going to buy anyway, that number would sit near zero. It doesn't, which is what a holdout comparison is designed to surface — reach that wasn't already going to convert.

A DTC brand running the same structure — say, a home or personal-care brand like Beardbrand or Epic Gardening — would look at that same new-to-brand split before deciding whether display spend earned its keep, rather than trusting the last-click report that credited it with everything or nothing.

When the number is bad news

Sometimes the holdout comparison shows no meaningful lift. That's a finding, not a failure of the test. Before assuming the campaign doesn't work, check three things: was the holdout group actually clean, or did retargeting leak into it; was the flight long enough to see behavior change, not just impressions; and was the audience big enough that the difference could show up statistically rather than getting lost in noise.

If those check out and the lift still isn't there, the honest move is to stop, not to keep spending on a story that isn't true. Every change we make carries three things before it runs: the evidence behind it, a measurement plan, and a rollback trigger. That third piece exists specifically for this moment — a pre-agreed line that says when to pull back, decided before anyone had an emotional stake in the result.

The common mistake — including ones we've made

The most common mistake is treating a rise in view-through conversions, or branded search volume, as proof of lift. Neither isolates cause from correlation. A shopper who was already going to buy will generate both.

We've made a version of this mistake ourselves: early on, reporting DSP-influenced sales that sponsored ads had already claimed credit for, because the two weren't reconciled in the same place. The fix wasn't a better dashboard — it was moving the reconciliation into AMC, where a purchase can only belong to one channel's incremental story at a time. Any report that shows DSP and sponsored ads each claiming full credit for the same period is double-counting, not double the result.

Common questions

Is "pride brand lift chairs" a real, common search?

It's likely two intents merging in a search box: people looking for Pride Mobility's power lift recliners, and advertisers researching brand lift measurement. The word overlap is coincidental. If you landed here wanting a recliner, Pride Mobility's own site and specialist retailers like My Mobility Store or US Medical Supplies carry the actual product line and pricing.

How is brand lift different from a brand awareness survey?

A survey asks people what they remember or feel. Brand lift measurement compares actual behavior — purchases, new-to-brand rate, search activity — between an exposed group and a holdout that saw nothing. Surveys measure recall; holdouts measure whether the ad caused a behavior change that wouldn't have happened otherwise.

Can Sponsored Products or Sponsored Brands measure lift the same way?

Sponsored ads are built around last-click and view-through attribution, which credits whoever was closest to the sale, not whoever caused it. You can still run a holdout against sponsored ads, but the honest read only happens once both DSP and sponsored exposures are reconciled in the same place — otherwise both channels claim the same purchase.

How big does a holdout group need to be to trust the result?

There's no single number — it depends on baseline conversion rate, spend level, and how long the flight runs. A holdout that's too small will show noise that looks like lift, or hide real lift in statistical uncertainty. Any vendor running these tests should be able to tell you the sample size and confidence level behind a specific readout before you act on it.

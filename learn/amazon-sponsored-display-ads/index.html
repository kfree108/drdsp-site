What Amazon Sponsored Display ads actually are

Sponsored Display is Amazon's self-service display ad product, sold through the same ad console as Sponsored Products and Sponsored Brands but built for retargeting and audience or product-based reach instead of keywords. You set a daily budget and a cost-per-click or viewable-cost-per-thousand bid, and Amazon shows your ad to shoppers who've viewed your listing, a competitor's, or a related category — on Amazon and on a range of third-party sites and apps.

Amazon recently folded Sponsored Display into a broader 'display ads' hub that also houses Amazon DSP workflows in the same console. That's a naming change, not a product change — campaigns you already built keep running through Campaign Manager exactly as before. But it explains why so many searches for 'Sponsored Display' now land on pages that also mention Amazon DSP: they are not the same thing. Sponsored Display is the low-budget, self-service version. Amazon DSP is the full programmatic platform, bought across and beyond Amazon's own properties, usually run by a specialist team rather than set up from a dashboard. More on that split below.

The three targeting types, and who's actually allowed to use each one

Sponsored Display doesn't target keywords. It targets people and products, and eligibility differs depending on whether you're a vendor or a Brand Registry seller. This trips people up more than any other part of the setup — you open 'Audiences,' expect interest-based targeting, and it isn't there, because you're a seller rather than a vendor.

Views remarketing is the one most sellers reach for first because it works: showing your product again to someone who looked and left converts better than cold traffic, almost by definition. Product targeting is the sharper tool — you can put your ad directly on a competitor's listing, or on a complementary product, without touching a single keyword.

A worked example: what a $50-a-day budget actually buys

Amazon's own starting-point guidance for small and mid-sized advertisers is a daily budget of $40 to $60, with a default bid of $1 for CPC campaigns or $5 for vCPM campaigns. Here's what that means in practice rather than in theory.

At a $50 daily budget on a $1 CPC bid, you're not guaranteed 50 clicks a day — your final CPC moves with the auction. On a competitive ASIN you might pay $1.20 and get roughly 40 clicks; on a quiet niche you might pay $0.60 and get over 80. On a $5 vCPM bid, that same $50 buys around 10,000 viewable impressions if your bid holds — a reach play, not a click play. Which one to pick depends on whether you're buying attention or buying intent.

For a sense of scale once display spend moves off the self-service console entirely and into Amazon DSP: across 30 of those advertisers in July 2026, the book delivered 6.04x return on ad spend, 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM and a blended $1.42 cost-per-click. Different platform, different inventory, different math — but it shows how far the same cost-per-click and cost-per-thousand logic stretches once budget and reach both scale up.

The mistakes that actually cost money, including one we've made

  • Reading 'sales' in the Sponsored Display dashboard as proof the ad worked. Last-click attribution credits whichever ad the shopper clicked last, even if they were already going to buy. We've made this mistake ourselves — reading a spike in attributed sales as incremental, when a chunk of it was shoppers who'd have converted anyway through Sponsored Products or organic search. The only way to know for sure is a holdout: hold a matched group of shoppers out of the campaign and compare, not read a bigger number in a report.
  • Setting a vCPM bid too low and getting no delivery, then assuming the audience is too small. Check the bid before you touch the targeting.
  • Leaving views remarketing running for months on the same creative. The audience refreshes every 30 days; the ad doesn't, unless you change it.
  • Trying to select 'Audience interests' as a seller and assuming the account is broken. It isn't — that option is vendor-only.

If the number in front of you is bad news — CTR collapsed, ACOS spiked, delivery stopped — check placement before you touch the bid. Amazon's placement report shows whether the drop is happening on-Amazon or off-Amazon, and off-Amazon inventory usually needs a different bid, not a paused campaign.

Where Sponsored Display ends and Amazon DSP begins

Sponsored Display is bought in the same console as Sponsored Products, funded from the same ad account, capped at whatever daily budget you set, and limited mostly to Amazon's own inventory plus some off-Amazon syndication. Amazon DSP is a different order of platform: it buys display, video and audio across Amazon-owned properties like Twitch and Fire TV, plus a much wider pool of exchanges, using Amazon's first-party shopping signals rather than just your own product's view history. It's typically bought through a seat, not a self-service console, and usually run by an in-house team or a specialist agency rather than set up in an afternoon.

The two overlap in one way that matters: both can end up claiming credit for the same sale. A shopper who saw a DSP video ad and later clicked a Sponsored Display retargeting ad before buying will often show up as a win in both reports. Reconciling that — in Amazon Marketing Cloud, where DSP and sponsored ads activity can be joined at the event level — is the only honest way to say whether display added a sale or just relabeled one already coming. At the scale where that reconciliation starts to matter, Full Circle and reMKTR run Amazon DSP across a group of 70+ brands, precisely because the question stops being 'did the ad get clicked' and becomes 'did the ad change the outcome.'

Which one you should actually pick

Sponsored Display suits sellers and vendors who want simple, low-budget retargeting inside the console they already use for Sponsored Products — no minimum spend, fast setup, solid for bringing back shoppers who looked and left. It stops being enough once you need off-Amazon reach, video or audio, or a real answer on incrementality — that's Amazon DSP territory, usually run as a managed service.

Common questions

Is Amazon Sponsored Display the same as Amazon DSP?

No. Sponsored Display is a self-service ad product inside Amazon's regular ad console, bid on CPC or vCPM with no minimum spend. Amazon DSP is a separate, larger programmatic platform for display, video and audio, bought through a seat and normally run by a specialist team, not set up from a dashboard in an afternoon.

What happened to Sponsored Display — did Amazon get rid of it?

No, Amazon renamed the entry point. Sponsored Display now sits inside a unified 'display ads' hub that also holds DSP workflows, but existing Sponsored Display campaigns keep running exactly as before through Campaign Manager. If you're creating a new campaign, look for 'Display' rather than 'Sponsored Display' in the campaign type list.

Can any seller use Sponsored Display's audience interest targeting?

No. Audience interests are vendor-only. Brand Registry sellers get product targeting and views remarketing, which cover retargeting and competitor placement, but not the broader interest-based audience option.

How do I know if my Sponsored Display sales are incremental?

The dashboard can't tell you. Last-click attribution credits whoever clicked last, whether or not the ad actually changed their decision. A holdout test, where a matched group of shoppers is excluded from the campaign and compared against the rest, is the only reliable way to isolate what the ad added.

What budget do I need to start Sponsored Display?

There's no minimum. Amazon's own starting guidance for small and mid-sized advertisers is a daily budget of $40 to $60, with a default bid of $1 for CPC or $5 for vCPM — adjust from there based on delivery and results.

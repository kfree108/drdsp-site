What Is Programmatic Advertising?

The short answer

Programmatic advertising is buying digital ad space through automated, real-time auctions instead of manually negotiated orders. A demand-side platform bids on each ad impression in milliseconds, using rules you set — audience, budget, placement — and the ad serves the instant it wins.

Programmatic advertising, defined without the jargon

Programmatic advertising means software buys the ad, not a person. An advertiser sets rules — who to reach, what to pay, where the ad can run — and a piece of software called a demand-side platform (DSP) enters an auction for every single ad impression, thousands of times a second, across the web.

The alternative is the old way: a media buyer calls a publisher, negotiates a rate, signs an insertion order, and the ad runs for a fixed number of weeks regardless of who actually sees it. Programmatic replaces that negotiation with an auction that happens in the time it takes a page to load.

Note on terms: when we say DSP here we mean demand-side platform, the software that buys ad impressions. It has nothing to do with Delivery Service Partner, the Amazon courier franchise — same three letters, unrelated businesses.

How it works, step by step, with real numbers

Here's the mechanics in order. A shopper loads a page. The publisher's supply-side platform (SSP) sends that ad slot to auction. Every connected DSP evaluates the impression against its advertisers' rules — audience match, budget remaining, creative fit — and submits a bid in real time. The highest bid wins, the ad serves, and the whole thing is over before the page finishes rendering.

What that looks like at scale: across 30 of Full Circle and reMKTR's Amazon DSP advertisers in July 2026, the book delivered 6.04x return on ad spend, 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM, and a blended $1.42 cost-per-click. Cost per acquisition landed at $5.49 across 57,137 attributed purchases, 20.1% of them from shoppers new to the brand. Those are the outputs of the process above, repeated billions of times.

If you're asking how to actually do this: pick the goal (awareness, consideration, or retargeting non-converters), pick the DSP, define the audience and budget guardrails, let the auction run, then measure. The measurement step is where most guides stop — and where most of the real work is.

The vocabulary: DSP, SSP, ad exchange, RTB

  • DSP (demand-side platform): software the advertiser uses to bid. Amazon DSP, The Trade Desk, DV360 are examples.
  • SSP (supply-side platform): software the publisher uses to sell inventory and run the auction.
  • Ad exchange: the marketplace connecting DSPs and SSPs — think of it as the trading floor.
  • RTB (real-time bidding): the auction mechanism itself — bids submitted and resolved in the time it takes a page to load.
  • Header bidding: a publisher technique that lets multiple SSPs compete for the same impression simultaneously, rather than waiting in a sequential chain.

Programmatic display, video, and audio are all bought this way. A display ad is the creative; programmatic is the buying mechanism underneath it — the two get confused constantly, and they're not the same thing.

The mistake almost every brand makes with programmatic

Last-click attribution gives sponsored ads credit for a purchase that display already influenced, or vice versa. It cannot tell you whether the display spend added an incremental sale or just touched a shopper who was going to buy anyway. This isn't a Dr. DSP talking point — it's a structural limit of last-click measurement, and it never could answer that question, on any platform.

We've made this mistake ourselves: crediting a DSP retargeting push for purchases that sponsored ads had already earned through last-click, because the two systems don't talk to each other by default. The fix is a holdout group — a matched set of shoppers who see no DSP ads — compared against the exposed group, reconciled in Amazon Marketing Cloud so DSP and sponsored ads stop double-counting the same shopper. That's the only honest way to say display added anything.

Brands like HexClad or Beardbrand running programmatic display against cart abandoners or category shoppers who haven't converted are a common, legitimate use case. The mistake isn't running that campaign — it's trusting last-click to tell you if it worked.

When the numbers look wrong: what to check first

If CPMs look high relative to a $4.00 benchmark, check the inventory tier first — open exchange, private marketplace, and programmatic guaranteed deals price very differently, and mixing them without labeling skews the average. If ROAS looks low, check whether frequency capping is actually on; unlimited frequency burns budget on shoppers who've already seen the ad a dozen times.

If a campaign that looked good in the DSP dashboard doesn't show up in incremental lift once you run it through AMC, that's not a failure to hide — it's the answer. Don't rerun the same creative with a bigger budget hoping the number moves. Change one thing, write down what evidence justified the change, and set a rollback trigger before it goes live. If it still doesn't move after that, the honest read is that the audience or channel wasn't the constraint.

Common questions

What is a simple example of programmatic advertising?

A shopper browses a category page for garden tools, leaves without buying, then sees a display ad for that same product a day later on a news site. A DSP bid for that shopper's impression in an auction based on the browsing signal, at whatever CPM won the slot, and served the ad automatically — no human picked that placement.

Is programmatic advertising the same as display advertising?

No. Display is a creative format — a banner or video unit. Programmatic is the buying mechanism underneath it. Display ads can be bought programmatically or through a direct, manually negotiated deal; most large display buys today are programmatic, but the two terms describe different layers of the stack.

How much does programmatic advertising cost?

It's usually priced by CPM (cost per thousand impressions), and that CPM varies by inventory tier, competition, and audience scarcity — there's no single number. Managed programmatic services on top of that media cost are typically quoted per seat, banded by spend, or as a percentage of spend; check any vendor's current pricing directly rather than trusting a listicle, since these figures change often and vary by region.

Is Amazon DSP programmatic advertising?

Yes. Amazon DSP is a demand-side platform — the software an advertiser uses to bid programmatically for display, video, and audio impressions both on and off Amazon. It's one DSP among several; The Trade Desk and DV360 are others operating on the same auction principle.

What's the biggest mistake beginners make with programmatic advertising?

Judging success on last-click attribution alone. It tells you who touched a sale last, not whether the ad caused the sale. Without a holdout group or a clean-room reconciliation like AMC, it's easy to keep paying for impressions that were never doing anything.

What Epsilon Retail Media Actually Is

Epsilon Retail Media is a technology and data business, not a place brands go to buy ads. It sells identity resolution, predictive audiences and clean-room infrastructure to retailers who want to build or run a retail media network without building all of that themselves from scratch. Epsilon is part of Publicis Groupe.

The distinction matters because "retail media network" gets used loosely. Amazon Ads, Walmart Connect and Target Roundel are retail media networks — retailers selling ad space against their own first-party shopper data. Epsilon isn't on that list. It's closer to a supplier: the identity layer and clean room a retailer licenses so it can launch, or improve, its own network.

That's a real and growing market. U.S. retail media spend is heading toward $60 billion this year and is projected to reach $100 billion by 2028, per eMarketer figures Epsilon itself cites — which is exactly why retailers well beyond the household names are getting into the business, and why the identity and data layer underneath them has become its own category.

The Three Layers Behind Every Retail Media Buy

Every retail media transaction — whether it runs on Amazon, Walmart Connect, or a regional retailer running an Epsilon-style stack underneath — involves three separate parties doing three separate jobs. Knowing which one you're actually talking to saves a lot of confused sales calls.

A Worked Example: What a Retail Media ROAS Number Actually Tells You

Epsilon's own materials cite a L'Occitane campaign that returned $21 for every $1 spent, and a Walgreens network that generated $10 million in a single quarter. Both are real numbers from real campaigns. Neither tells you the thing you actually need to know: how much of that would have happened anyway.

A $21:1 return sounds enormous until you ask what it's measured against. Last-click attribution credits an ad for a sale it might have gotten with or without the ad — someone who already had the item in their cart, or was already a loyal buyer, still gets counted as "won" the moment they click. That's not dishonest. It's just not proof of incrementality. Only a holdout group or a matched control shows what happens in the ad's absence.

Here's what that discipline looks like on a real book. Across 30 advertisers we managed in July 2026, the number that mattered wasn't just a 6.04x return on ad spend — it was that figure reconciled against 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM and a blended $1.42 cost-per-click, checked in Amazon Marketing Cloud so display and sponsored ads weren't quietly claiming the same sale twice. That reconciliation step is the part most retail media reporting skips.

The Common Mistake: Reading Every Placement as if It Works Alone

On-site, off-site and in-store placements each report their own numbers, and each one looks good in isolation. The mistake — one we've made too, early in a program before we tightened the process — is launching an off-site placement, watching its ROAS climb, and not checking whether it's simply pulling credit from a sponsored search campaign that would have closed the same sale anyway.

Epsilon's own guide names identity resolution and clean rooms as the fix for fragmented data, and that's correct as far as it goes. But identity resolution only tells you it's the same shopper across channels. It doesn't tell you which channel caused the sale, or whether either one did. That's a measurement question, not an identity question, and it needs a different tool: a holdout or matched control, run before spend scales, not after.

When the Number Doesn't Hold Up

If a retail media test comes back with a strong ROAS but you can't tell whether it's incremental, don't kill the placement and don't scale it either. Run it as a holdout: take a matched slice of the audience, withhold the ad, and compare. If the "won" group and the holdout group convert at close to the same rate, the ad didn't do much — regardless of what last-click said.

The same logic applies if a network's reported revenue jumps but attributed purchases don't move with it, or if new-to-brand share gets buried instead of reported. Those are the questions worth asking before signing, not after.

Every change we make carries three things before it runs: the evidence behind it, a measurement plan, and a rollback trigger. If a test fails that bar, it doesn't run yet — that's the part of the process that keeps a good-looking number from turning into a bad quarter.

Where This Leaves a Brand Choosing Where to Spend

If you're a retailer building a network, Epsilon's identity and clean-room stack is a legitimate option worth evaluating against alternatives — it's built specifically for that job. If you're a brand deciding where to put retail media dollars, Epsilon isn't who you call. You're calling Amazon, Walmart, Target, CVS or whichever retailer's network sits on top of Epsilon or a competitor's technology.

Common questions

Is Epsilon Retail Media the same thing as Amazon Ads or Amazon DSP?

No. Epsilon Retail Media is identity and data infrastructure that retailers license to build their own networks. Amazon Ads, including Amazon DSP, is Amazon running its own retail media network end to end — it doesn't need Epsilon's technology underneath it.

Who owns Epsilon?

Epsilon is part of Publicis Groupe, which acquired the company in 2019. It operates as an identity and data business serving retailers, brands and agencies, not as a retail media network in its own right.

Can a brand buy ads directly through Epsilon?

Generally no. Brands buy media from the retailer operating the network — a pharmacy chain, a grocery retailer, a home improvement chain — and Epsilon's technology, where used, sits underneath that retailer's platform handling identity and measurement.

What's the difference between retail media and commerce media?

Retail media is retailer-specific: a store monetizes its own first-party shopper data for brand advertisers. Commerce media extends that same model to non-retail businesses with valuable audience data — airlines, hotels, financial services — that want to create similar ad opportunities.

How do I know if a retail media network's reported ROAS is trustworthy?

Ask one question: is this last-click, or is it measured against a holdout or matched control? Last-click numbers can be real and still overstate incrementality, because they credit sales that might have happened without the ad. Only a control group proves what the ad actually added.

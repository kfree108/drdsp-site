The actual difference, not the marketing-blog version

Display ads look like ads. They're banners, custom HTML units, or video creative that visually contrasts with the page around them — bold colors, a clear border, an obvious call to action. Native ads are built to disappear into their surroundings: a recommended-content tile on a publisher's homepage, an in-feed unit on a social platform, a sponsored listing that reads like a product recommendation rather than a pitch.

That's the look-and-feel difference every article on this topic covers. The part most of them skip is the measurement difference, which matters more. Display is usually judged on pre-click behavior: impressions delivered, viewability, reach against a target audience. Native is usually judged on post-click behavior: did the person actually engage with what they landed on. Comparing a native CTR to a display CTR without accounting for that is comparing two different questions, not two answers to the same one.

Inside Amazon DSP specifically, both formats sit in the same buy. You can run standard display banners, custom image or HTML creative, native recommendation formats, video, and audio from one seat, targeting the same audiences across Amazon-owned inventory and third-party exchanges. The format choice doesn't require a separate platform or a separate contract — it's a line item decision, not a channel decision.

A worked example: what a blended book actually returns

Numbers help more than definitions. Across 30 advertisers running Amazon DSP in July 2026, the book delivered a 6.04x return on ad spend across 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM, with a blended $1.42 cost-per-click and a blended $5.49 cost-per-acquisition across 57,137 attributed purchases — 20.1% of them from shoppers new to the brand.

That's a mixed pool of formats, not a pure native or pure display number, and that's the point. In practice almost nobody runs one format in isolation. A brand runs display for reach against a cold audience, then layers native placements to carry people who've already shown interest into content, then retargets the same pool with display again closer to the product page. The blended CPM and CPC above is what that kind of mixed strategy actually costs and returns at scale — not a theoretical rate card number, an operating one.

The mistake is assuming the format that shows the last click did the work. In a sequence like the one above, display usually opens the funnel and native or search usually closes it. Last-click attribution hands all the credit to whichever step happened last, regardless of what actually moved the shopper.

The mistake almost everyone makes comparing the two

The common comparison — "native gets a higher CTR than display, so native wins" — measures the wrong thing. Native ads are shown in feeds people are already scrolling through with intent to engage; display ads are shown alongside content people are reading for other reasons. Higher engagement on native doesn't mean display added nothing. It means they're doing different jobs in the funnel.

We've made this mistake ourselves: running a display retargeting step after a native or content touch and assuming the display step earned the conversions that followed it, because last-click attribution said so. Last-click attribution cannot prove incrementality and never could — it can only tell you which touch happened closest to the sale, not which touch caused it.

The fix is a holdout or matched control: hold a comparable audience out of the display step entirely and compare conversion rates between the exposed group and the holdout. If the exposed group doesn't convert meaningfully better, the display step isn't adding anything, no matter what the last-click report says. We reconcile this in Amazon Marketing Cloud, where DSP and sponsored ads stop double-counting each other — it's the only honest way to say whether a format actually added sales rather than just showing up at the end of a path someone was already on.

When each format actually earns its budget

  • Cold reach and brand awareness: display wins — it's built to be seen, not necessarily clicked, and it scales cheaply against broad audiences.
  • Retargeting a previous visitor: display wins — a shopper who's already interested doesn't need to be convinced with soft-sell content, they need a reminder.
  • Driving a specific action after the click: native wins — sign-ups, content reads, and considered purchases respond better to a format that doesn't feel like an interruption.
  • Tight budget, need lower cost-per-action: native often wins — the post-click focus tends to produce a lower effective CPA when the creative and landing experience are aligned.
  • Proving the channel actually worked: neither format wins by default — that requires a holdout, not a format choice.

When the numbers don't back up the story

If native's CTR looks strong but attributed sales aren't moving, check the attribution window before touching the creative — a native placement optimized for a 7-day click window will look worse under a 1-day window, and vice versa for display. If display CPMs are climbing without a CTR problem, check frequency; you may be paying to show the same banner to the same person for the fifth time that week.

If a format looks like it's underperforming, don't kill it off a single week of last-click numbers. Every change we make carries three things before it runs: the evidence behind it, a measurement plan, and a rollback trigger. That's what separates a real optimization from a reaction to noise. A format that looks weak under last-click attribution can still be the one holding the funnel open — you won't know until you test it with a holdout, not a dashboard glance.

Which one you should actually pick

If your goal is pure reach or retargeting a warm list, display does that job well and cheaply. If your goal is a considered action after the click, native tends to earn it back faster. Neither format proves itself through attribution — only a holdout does. Common questions

Is native advertising the same thing as content marketing?

No. Content marketing is owned content you publish yourself. Native advertising is paid placement — you're paying a platform or publisher to put your content or promoted listing into their feed, formatted to match their editorial style. The overlap is the soft-sell approach; the difference is who's paying for the placement.

Does Amazon DSP support native ad formats, or just display banners?

Amazon DSP supports both. You can run standard display, custom image or HTML creative, native recommendation formats, video, and audio from the same seat, targeting the same audience pools across Amazon-owned and third-party inventory. The choice is a creative and placement decision, not a separate platform.

Which format gets a better click-through rate, native or display?

Native formats generally show higher click-through rates than standard display banners, because they're placed inside content someone is already engaged with rather than alongside it. But CTR alone doesn't tell you which format drove a sale — it tells you which format got clicked, and those aren't the same question.

Can I run native and display in the same campaign?

Yes, and most advertisers who use either format seriously end up running both — display to build reach and stay top of mind, native to carry engaged shoppers into content or a considered purchase decision. They're complementary steps in a sequence more often than they're competing choices.

How do I actually know if display added sales rather than just brand awareness noise?

Run a holdout: keep a comparable audience out of the display exposure entirely and compare conversion rates between the exposed group and the holdout. If the two groups convert about the same, the display spend isn't adding incremental sales, regardless of what a last-click attribution report shows.

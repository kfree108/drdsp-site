What H-E-B Retail Media Actually Is

H-E-B Retail Media is the advertising arm of H-E-B, the privately held Texas grocery chain founded in 1905 and headquartered in San Antonio. It is a retailer selling ad space to the brands that already stock its 455+ stores — not Amazon's Demand-Side Platform, and not the Delivery Service Partner courier franchise, both of which share the DSP or delivery-adjacent naming that trips people up.

The network sells four kinds of placement: in-store (shelf blades, digital service screens, fuel pump screens), onsite (homepage carousels, shoppable recipes, digital end caps, category banners on heb.com and the My H-E-B app), offsite (programmatic display and social media bought using H-E-B's shopper data), and email (personalized sends to the retailer's own customer list). Like most grocery retail media, it exists because H-E-B holds first-party purchase data most advertisers can't buy anywhere else — what a Texas household actually buys, how often, and whether they shop online, in-store, or both.

The Four Channels, What Each One Actually Does

Each channel plays a different role and gets measured differently. Onsite tends to report the strongest numbers because it's shown to people already browsing to buy — that's not the same as saying it did the most work. In-store and offsite build reach that onsite doesn't get credited for, which is exactly why judging the whole program by the easiest-to-measure channel is the most common mistake advertisers make here.

The breakdown below is the structure, not a rate card — H-E-B doesn't publish pricing for any of these formats. Ask for a rate card broken out by placement type rather than a single blended CPM; grocery retail media networks typically price in-store and onsite differently, and lumping them together hides which channel you're actually paying for.

How the Numbers Are Actually Measured

Grocery retail media reports onsite sales the way most retail media does: last-click, inside its own walled garden. If a shopper saw a shelf blade, then bought the product that week, the network credits the ad. That's attribution, not incrementality — it doesn't tell you whether the shopper would have bought anyway.

The only way to know is a holdout: split otherwise-similar households or stores into an exposed group and a matched control, then measure the delta. This is the same discipline we've applied to a different platform entirely — across 30 Amazon DSP advertisers in July 2026, that book delivered 6.04x return on ad spend, 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM and a blended $1.42 cost-per-click, measured against holdouts and reconciled in Amazon Marketing Cloud rather than taken on last-click alone. H-E-B doesn't publish whether its reported lift is holdout-tested or last-click. Ask the rep directly before you scale a budget on it.

The Mistake Most Brands Make With This Data

Judging the whole spend by the onsite number, because it's the only one that comes with the friendliest ROAS. In-store and offsite media cost money too, and starving them to chase the onsite number that looks best on a slide doesn't mean the program is working better — it means you're measuring less of it.

We've made a version of this mistake ourselves on Amazon: treating a strong sponsored-products number as proof display added nothing, when the two channels were competing for credit on the same last-click sale. The fix isn't a different metric. It's separating the channels in a controlled test before deciding where the next dollar goes.

When the Numbers Don't Match What You See Elsewhere

If the lift H-E-B reports doesn't match your own retailer scorecard or syndicated data, check three things before calling the rep. First, the attribution window — in-store and onsite windows are rarely the same length, and a longer window inflates credit. Second, whether a promotion or price cut ran the same week; retail media and trade promotion overlap constantly and neither side subtracts the other's effect. Third, whether the comparison period is seasonally matched — Texas grocery sales move with heat, hurricane season, and school calendars in ways a flat year-over-year comparison misses.

Any advertiser running paid media, on H-E-B or anywhere else, should require three things before a change goes live: the evidence behind it, a measurement plan, and a rollback trigger. If a rep can't answer those three questions before you approve a budget increase, that's the actual problem — not the number that disappointed you.

Common questions

Is H-E-B Retail Media the same thing as Amazon DSP?

No. H-E-B Retail Media is the Texas grocery chain's own advertising network, sold to brands stocked in H-E-B stores. Amazon DSP is Amazon's programmatic buying platform for display, video and audio. Different retailer, different shopper data, different contract — they don't share a login or a measurement system.

How much does H-E-B Retail Media cost?

H-E-B doesn't publish a rate card. Ask for pricing broken out by placement type — in-store, onsite, offsite and email are typically priced differently rather than under one blended CPM. Get the structure in writing before you commit to a program-level budget.

Do I have to already sell my product in H-E-B stores to advertise with H-E-B Retail Media?

The network's own materials describe it as a tool for brands H-E-B already stocks, which is standard for grocery retail media generally. That restriction isn't spelled out in detail publicly, so confirm it directly with an account rep before assuming either way.

Does a strong onsite ROAS from H-E-B mean the campaign is working?

It means the onsite channel is working, or at least reporting well on last-click. It doesn't tell you what the in-store and offsite spend contributed, and it doesn't tell you whether the sale would have happened without the ad. Those require a holdout test, not a bigger onsite number.

Can I run H-E-B Retail Media and Amazon advertising at the same time?

Yes, and many brands do, since the two reach different shopper populations — H-E-B's is Texas grocery shoppers, Amazon's is a national ecommerce audience. Treat them as separate budgets with separate measurement; there's no shared attribution system, so combining their reported numbers into one ROAS figure will overstate what either channel actually did.

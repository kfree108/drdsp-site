Two Completely Different 'DSP' Stories

"Amazon DSP" points to two unrelated things, and if you searched "amazon dsp closing" you could mean either one. The first is Delivery Service Partners — the courier franchises that deliver Amazon packages. They're fighting proposed New York City legislation, the Delivery Protection Act, which could force some of them to close and have their work absorbed directly into Amazon. It's a real labor and subcontracting fight playing out at City Hall, with Teamsters, chamber of commerce presidents, and DSP owners testifying about licensing, direct-hire mandates, and personal loan guarantees.

The second is the Demand-Side Platform — Amazon's programmatic buying tool for display, video, and audio ads. Nothing about that platform is closing. But individual campaigns, line items, and deals inside it close constantly, for reasons that have nothing to do with Amazon's business health. This page covers the advertising platform from here on. If you came for the courier legislation: as of now, the bill has co-sponsors but hasn't passed, and the dispute is over whether Delivery Service Partners can keep subcontracting the work at all — not whether they already have.

If You Run Ads: What 'Closing' Means Inside a DSP Campaign

Inside the ad platform, "closing" almost always means one line item or deal stopped spending — not that your account, or Amazon's DSP business, shut down. The console rarely uses the word "closed." It says no delivery, ended, or the spend graph just goes flat. Six things cause that, and they're worth checking in order before assuming something broke.

  • Budget cap reached — the line item spent its daily or lifetime cap and resumes next cycle.
  • Flight end date passed — the order was scheduled to end, and did.
  • Deal or PO expired — a negotiated deal ran past its term and needs renewal.
  • Frequency cap saturation — the audience already saw the maximum allowed impressions.
  • Audience exhausted — a narrow segment ran out of eligible, in-cap users.
  • Creative or policy hold — an asset got flagged and needs review before it serves again.

A Worked Example: Why a Line Item Actually Stopped

Here's the arithmetic, using real numbers instead of a hypothetical. Across 30 advertisers we managed in July 2026, the book ran at a $4.00 CPM and delivered 78.4 million impressions with a blended $1.42 CPC, landing at 6.04x return on ad spend. Cost per acquisition came in at $5.49 across 57,137 attributed purchases, 20.1% of them new to the brand.

Take a line item budgeted at $20,000 for the month at that $4.00 CPM. That buys roughly 5 million impressions if nothing interrupts it. If it goes quiet on day 22 instead of day 30, the first question isn't "did Amazon shut something down" — it's whether CPMs came in lower than modeled and the budget paced faster, or whether a frequency cap capped the eligible audience early. Both look identical on a spend graph. Only the delivery diagnostics tell you which one happened.

The Common Mistake — And One We've Made Ourselves

The common mistake is reading a last-click dashboard, seeing DSP-assisted conversions drop, and pulling the line item before checking whether anything actually changed. Last-click attribution can't prove a display ad caused a sale — it can only show one happened before one. That's not the same claim, and treating it as one leads to pausing line items that were working and leaving underperforming ones running. We've done this ourselves, moved too fast off a soft weekly dip.

The fix is a holdout or matched control, reconciled against sponsored ads in Amazon Marketing Cloud so the two channels stop taking credit for the same shopper. That's slower than reading a dashboard. It's also the only honest way to know whether "closing" a line item costs you incremental sales or just removes noise.

What To Do When The Closure Is Bad News

If a line item closed and shouldn't have, work through it in order: confirm flight dates and budget caps first — those close things silently, by design. Check for a policy or creative hold next; Amazon flags this in the line item status, but it's easy to miss in a busy console. If none of that explains it, pull the delivery diagnostics report before touching anything else. Before any change goes live, it should carry three things: the evidence behind it, a measurement plan, and a rollback trigger.

If the closure was performance-driven — someone or something paused it because it looked like it stopped working — don't just restart it blind. Check what a holdout or matched control said about that line item before it closed. If there wasn't one running, that's the real problem, and it's bigger than one closed campaign.

Common questions

Is Amazon DSP, the advertising platform, shutting down?

No. Nothing about Amazon's Demand-Side Platform is closing. If you saw "DSP closing" in the news, it's almost certainly the New York City legislation affecting Delivery Service Partners, the courier franchises, not the ad platform.

Why did my Amazon DSP line item stop delivering?

Check the flight end date and budget cap first, since both close a line item silently by design. Then check for a frequency cap, an exhausted audience, or a creative and policy hold. The delivery diagnostics report will usually name the exact cause.

What's the difference between Amazon DSP and a Delivery Service Partner?

They share an acronym and nothing else. DSP as Demand-Side Platform is Amazon's programmatic ad-buying tool. DSP as Delivery Service Partner is the courier franchise model that delivers Amazon packages. One is under legislative pressure in New York City; the other isn't.

Can Amazon pause a DSP account without warning?

A specific line item can go on hold for a flagged creative or a policy issue, and that will show in the status field. A full account pause is rare and usually tied to a compliance or billing issue, not performance.

How do I know if pausing a line item actually saved money, or just hid a channel that was working?

Last-click reporting can't answer that question — it wasn't built to. Run a holdout or matched control and reconcile the result against sponsored ads in Amazon Marketing Cloud so the two channels aren't both claiming the same sale.

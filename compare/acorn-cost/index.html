The short answer

Acorn-i publishes no pricing on its site; the cost is quoted on a call, which is normal for a managed retail media partner. What decides your bill is the fee architecture — a fixed licence, a percentage of working media, or both — and whether your media budget is inclusive of those fees.

The only fee architecture in this category published in full

If you want to understand how retail media partners charge, the most useful document in the market is not a pricing page. It is Criteo's Specific Terms of Service for Commerce Max, which spells the structure out in plain contract language. Their own terms state that fees are the Demand Side Platform Fee and, if applicable, the Managed Service Fee, both calculated as a percentage of Working Media Spend, that working media is the cost payable to retailers or publishers for the inventory itself, and — the clause worth reading twice — that the budget outlined in the Order Form is inclusive of Service Fees.

Read that last part slowly. If the order form says, say, $100,000 a month and the fees sit inside it, your working media is not, in fact, $100,000. The same terms also describe a Retailer Audience Data Fee, charged as a flat CPM or a fixed percentage, and possible third-party technology fees on top.

None of that is unusual and none of it is wrong. It is simply the anatomy of a retail media invoice, written down by someone who had to. When you get an Acorn-i quote — or ours, or anyone's — map it onto the same four questions: what is the platform fee, what is the service fee, what does audience data cost, and is my stated budget inclusive or exclusive of all of it.

One vendor does publish, and it makes a useful yardstick

Skai is the exception in this set: real annual prices on their own pricing page, banded by how much media you run. Read on 20 August 2026: Skai from $114,000 a year for advertisers under four million dollars of annual spend, and $276,000, $504,000 and $756,000 as the bands rise to ten, twenty and thirty-five million — annual billing, with the commitment reviewable after the first three months.

Two honest uses for those numbers. First, they show what enterprise retail media software costs when it is not bundled into a service, which is the number a percentage-of-media quote is implicitly competing with. Second, they let you do the arithmetic in reverse: against four million dollars of annual media, $114,000 is roughly 2.9% of spend — a licence behaving like a percentage. Prices change, so check their page before you budget rather than trusting this paragraph in six months.

Quartile and Pacvue sit where Acorn-i sits: quote on a call. Pacvue's pricing URL returns a 404 today. The Trade Desk publishes nothing either — we looked.

The undisclosed point is bigger than the headline fee

Buyers negotiate the visible number and sign away the invisible one. The arithmetic is not complicated and almost nobody runs it on the call.

  • Media at, say, $100,000 a month: one percentage point of fee, $1,000 a month — $12,000 a year.
  • Three points, $36,000 a year, which is a headcount in most brands.
  • If the budget is fee-inclusive, every point also removes a point of working media, so the effect lands twice: you pay more and you buy less.

Which is why the slider above is not a price list. It is a shape. Move it to your real monthly media and look at the distance between a fixed licence and a percentage — that distance, not the logo, is what you are actually choosing between.

One thing we will not do on this page is attack percentage-of-media pricing. It is a perfectly rational way to sell managed media, several good companies charge it, and the alignment argument for it is real. The problem is never the model. The problem is a rate you were never told.

What the media itself costs, before anyone's fee

The fee conversation is only half the budget. Amazon DSP has its own cost layer that exists regardless of who runs it: inventory bought on a CPM basis, and Amazon audience segments that carry a cost per thousand impressions of their own — Acorn-i's own DSP writing describes exactly that. Managed-service arrangements through Amazon also carry minimum commitments, and those vary by market and by year, so ask rather than trust a number you read on a blog.

For scale, here is what our book actually clears. Across 30 advertisers in July 2026, pulled straight from the Amazon DSP API: 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM, a blended $1.42 cost per click, $5.49 cost per acquisition across 57,137 attributed purchases, and 6.04x return on ad spend portfolio-wide. Scope it honestly: that is 30 advertisers in one month, not all 109 seats we operate, and it is Amazon-attributed data — efficient buying, not proof of incrementality. Different categories land in different places.

The reason CPM matters to a cost page: a partner charging two points less on a fee while buying supply two dollars worse on the CPM has cost you money, not saved it. Compare the whole stack.

Whatever you buy — a seat, a service, or nothing — that number is the one it has to move, and a cheaper tool nobody has time to drive will not move it.

Common questions

How much does Acorn-i cost?

They do not publish a rate, so any figure you find online is someone's guess or someone's old invoice. Ask for the fee split — platform versus managed service — whether it is a percentage of working media or a fixed fee, and whether your stated budget is inclusive of fees. Those three answers determine the cost far more than the headline number.

Is a percentage of ad spend a bad way to be charged?

No. It scales with the work, it is common across this category, and we charge on media-related terms ourselves on other products. What is bad is not being told the rate, or discovering that the budget you approved was inclusive of fees you had not seen. Ask for the number in writing and model it at your real spend.

What minimum spend does Amazon DSP require?

Minimums exist for managed service and they move by market and by year, which is why we will not print one. Ask Amazon or your partner for the current figure in your marketplace. The more useful threshold is analytical: below a certain monthly spend, results are noise and a holdout will never reach significance, so you would be paying for a measurement you cannot act on.

Should I compare a software licence to a managed fee at all?

Only with the labour priced in. A licence assumes someone on your team runs the platform daily; a managed fee assumes someone else does. Add a realistic salary to the software column before you compare, and add the cost of the days when nobody has time to log in.

What Quartile is, in one paragraph

Quartile sells software that builds and optimises retail media campaigns at a very granular level — per-ASIN campaign structures, dynamic bidding on real-time data, automated placement adjustments — across Amazon sponsored ads and Amazon DSP, Walmart, Instacart and Criteo inventory, alongside Google, Meta and Microsoft on the direct-to-consumer side.

On its own site Quartile claims more than $2B in annual retail ad spend managed and over 5,300 customers. Pricing is not published, and there is no pricing page to check: quartile.com/pricing now redirects to the home page, and the site takes demo requests instead.

Note for anyone who arrived from an ambiguous search: DSP here means Demand-Side Platform — programmatic display, video and audio buying — not Delivery Service Partner, Amazon's courier franchise. This review is written by Dr. DSP, an Amazon DSP product from Full Circle, a full-service Amazon management company: $500M+ in managed spend across 100+ brands. We compete with Quartile on some accounts and lose to them on others; both of those facts inform this page.

What reviewers consistently praise

Read on 20 August 2026, Capterra showed Quartile at 4.5 out of 5 from 94 reviews. The themes repeat enough to be signal rather than noise.

  • The account teams. Reviewers describe managers as responsive and knowledgeable, treating the client's business as their own. In a category where software vendors often ship a login and a welcome email, this comes up again and again.
  • Automation that scales. Hourly bidding and bulk campaign management let small teams run structures they could not maintain by hand. Sellers with hundreds of ASINs are the clearest beneficiaries.
  • Granularity. Per-ASIN campaigns give precise control over where budget lands, which suits catalogues where products have genuinely different economics.
  • Time to result. Several reviewers report improved return within a couple of months of onboarding.

That is a real product with real advocates. Anyone comparing options should take the support quality seriously — it is the single most commonly praised attribute, and it is the attribute most often missing from cheaper software.

What reviewers complain about

The criticisms cluster just as tightly, and they are mostly the shadow of the strengths.

  • Complexity. One reviewer put it bluntly — there is no simple view of the metrics; the information is accurate but hard to handle without familiarity. Another described the end-user tool as looking dated.
  • Expertise required. Managing highly granular structures takes technical fluency. If the person operating it leaves, the structure remains and the understanding does not.
  • Rigidity. Some users find the AI's campaign construction inflexible when they want to build things its way rather than theirs.

None of that makes it a poor platform. It makes it a platform with a shape: it rewards teams who commit to its method, and frustrates teams who want to keep their own. Know which you are before you sign.

The question a bidding platform structurally cannot answer

Here is the thing that applies to every optimisation platform in this category, ours included unless we design around it. Automated bidding optimises toward the conversions the platform can see — and on Amazon, what the platform sees is last-touch attributed conversions. That is a fine objective for sponsored search, where the click and the purchase are tightly coupled. It is a much weaker objective for display.

Why: last-click attribution cannot prove incrementality and never could. Retargeting reliably produces the best reported return in any DSP account, because it puts impressions in front of shoppers who were already coming back. Optimise hard toward reported return and the algorithm will keep buying more of exactly that — a machine efficiently re-purchasing customers you already had, with a chart that looks like success.

What answers it: a holdout — withhold display from a matched region or ASIN set and compare against a control — and reconciliation in Amazon Marketing Cloud, where DSP impressions and sponsored-ads events land at event level and stop double-counting each other. Outside AMC, display and search each claim the same order and the two reports add up to a business you do not have.

This is not a criticism of Quartile specifically. It is the standing limitation of buying to a reported metric, and it is answerable by anyone willing to design the test. Ask them how they would.

A benchmark and a 30-day evaluation you can actually run

Carry a number into the demo. From a direct Amazon DSP API pull covering 30 advertisers in July 2026 — 30 of the 109 live seats our group operates, not the whole book: 6.04x return on ad spend, 78.4 million impressions at a $4.00 CPM, a blended $1.42 cost per click, $5.49 cost per acquisition across 57,137 attributed purchases, and 20.1% new-to-brand. Amazon-attributed data, one month, no guarantee attached to any of it.

Then run a structured evaluation rather than a vibe check:

  • Week 1. Get the proposed account structure in writing before anything is built. Ask what the platform will do automatically and what needs your sign-off.
  • Week 2. Pick one measurable question — for example, whether the deepest retargeting tier is incremental — and ask for a test design with a defined window and a success condition.
  • Week 3. Ask for the change log: every change made, with the reason and the expected effect. If nobody can produce one, you are buying a black box.
  • Week 4. Compare new-to-brand share against the previous period. If reported return went up while new-to-brand share fell, the account got more efficient at reaching people it already had.

What to do with this

Judge this on the job you actually need done, not the feature list. Pull your own search-term report for the last 90 days and total the spend against terms that produced no orders — on the account above that was 33.6% of everything spent. Then ask whether the thing you are about to buy closes that gap, or just shows it to you.

Common questions

Is Quartile good for Amazon DSP specifically?

It supports Amazon DSP alongside sponsored ads and several other retail networks, and its automation strength is real. The question to press on is measurement: what design proves the display was incremental rather than well-attributed. Any capable partner can answer that; make sure yours does before the first flight.

How much does Quartile cost?

No amount is published on their site — it routes to a demo request. The only pricing text Quartile publishes is in its terms of service, which describe tiers set by aggregate monthly ad spend, the number of channels managed, and/or the service level agreed, with the figures recorded in the order form. Ask which of those drives your quote, where the tier boundaries fall, what the minimum term is, and whether managed support sits inside the fee or beside it.

Is Quartile better than running Amazon DSP in-house?

Depends entirely on whether you have someone whose actual job is the platform. Software plus a competent full-time operator usually beats a service on cost at scale, and loses badly when that person is busy. Be honest about how many days a month your account will really get.

What does the complexity complaint mean in practice?

Granular per-ASIN structures produce a lot of objects to look at, and the reporting assumes fluency. Practically: budget onboarding time, name one person as the owner, and ask for a simplified reporting view up front. Teams that invest that time tend to be the ones writing the positive reviews.

Which should I shortlist alongside Quartile?

Skai if you want published enterprise pricing and an in-house trading model; Criteo Commerce Max if offsite retail media across many retailers is the job; an Amazon specialist or managed product if Amazon is the main event and you want the work done daily rather than driven by your team.
